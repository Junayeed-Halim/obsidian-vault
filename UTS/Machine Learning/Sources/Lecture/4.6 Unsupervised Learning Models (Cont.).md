# Probabilistic Generative Models

The previous section has introduced a few computational models to describe the data population by discovering intrinsic structures in the co-variation among the raw features. It turns out such methods can be considered as compression of the data — the extracted structure often corresponds to a low-dimensional subset of the high-dimensional input space. On the other hand, compression often implies loss of details. Hence the other side of the model is a choice of the uncertainty of the original signal given the compressed representation. These considerations point to the most natural language and theoretical framework for constructing and analysing such models — the probabilistic data models.

To be concrete, consider the motivating question: *what would it mean to fully understand the structure of a dataset?* This question was implicitly posed in the early days of computational learning theory (Valiant, 1984): the “teacher” in the learning paradigm transmits information to the “student”, subject to the following two protocols: i) the teacher can be queried for an EXAMPLE of the data, ii) the teacher can JUDGE whether a given sample is valid. A natural criterion for successful learning is that the student can eventually perform both of these tasks independently. The task is performed most naturally in a probabilistic framework: if the knowledge of the data distribution \(P(\cdot)\) is available, then the student can generate new examples by sampling from \(P\), and can judge the validity of a sample \(\mathbf{x}\) by evaluating \(P(\mathbf{x})\). In this vein, we first consider the fundamental questions to be answered by a probabilistic model: (Note that the discussion subsumes supervised learning: we can consider the distribution \(P\) to be over the joint space of inputs \(x\) and labels \(y\), and we will simplify the notation by using a single symbol to represent all observable variables \(P(x)\).)

## A Probabilistic Model of the Data
### Two Fundamental Quests

It is very useful to be specific about the notion “a probabilistic model of the data”: It refers to the knowledge of the probability distribution over the data space \(\mathcal{X}\). The knowledge comes in two forms:

- 

**Evaluation**: given a data point \(\mathbf{x}\), compute (or estimate) its probability \(p(\mathbf{x})\).

- 

**Sampling**: draw a fresh sample \(\mathbf{x}\) from the distribution \(p(\mathbf{x})\).

The literal meaning of the requirements is straightforward. However, a practical remark is necessary.

**Remark (Tractability).** In the context of data modelling, the means by which one fulfils these requirements must be computationally tractable. As a *counter*-example, modelling \(p(X)\) via the latent-variable marginalisation (the integration) produces a mathematically valid model, but this does *not* count as fulfilling the Evaluation requirement until a valid computational procedure for the integral is provided. The latent variable \(Z\) typically lives in a high-dimensional space with complicated dependence structure, making the integral analytically intractable.

Not every model supports both capabilities equally well. Some models (normalizing flows) offer exact likelihood evaluation and exact sampling. Others (GANs) produce high-quality samples but do not provide likelihoods at all. Still others (score-based models) can generate samples via an iterative process but represent the distribution only implicitly. Much of the history of deep generative modelling can be read as the search for architectures that strike the right balance between these two capabilities, computational tractability, and the quality of the learned representation.

## Why Probabilistic Models Matter

Beyond generation and evaluation, a model of \(p(\mathbf{x})\) serves as the foundation for a broader range of tasks.

- 

**Data augmentation and synthesis**: once we can sample from \(p(\mathbf{x})\), we can augment small datasets, synthesise test cases, or generate training data for downstream supervised models.

- 

**Compression**: Shannon’s source-coding theorem establishes that the optimal code length for \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(-\log_2 p(\mathbf{x})\) bits. A good probabilistic model is therefore a good compressor.

- 

**Generative classification**. A discriminative classifier directly models \(p(y \mid \mathbf{x})\). A generative classifier instead models the joint \(p(\mathbf{x}, y) = p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\, p(y)\) and recovers \(p(y \mid \mathbf{x})\) via Bayes’ theorem:

\[ p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\, p(y)}{p(\mathbf{x})}. \]

One class-conditional model \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\) is trained per class, and prediction is by the class that assigns the highest likelihood to \(\mathbf{x}\). This is the approach taken by Naïve Bayes classifiers, a classical and still practically useful family of methods.

- 

**Representation learning**. Training a generative model forces the network to capture the statistical regularities of \(\mathcal{X}\). The intermediate representations thereby learned often transfer well to downstream supervised tasks, even without any label signal.

### Latent Variable Models, Tractability, and the Equivalence Class of Hard Problems

A large family of probabilistic models introduces an auxiliary latent variable \(Z\) in the formulation of the data probability. The hypothesis is that each instance is only partially observed, i.e. an object of interest has its full information encoded in \((X=\mathbf{x}, Z=\mathbf{z})\), while only \(X=\mathbf{x}\) is observed. The so-called “latent variable” \(Z\) represents the unobserved underlying internal state of the object. The full probability is then interpreted as a *generative process*:

\[ \begin{align} Z &\sim p(Z), \\ X \mid Z &\sim p(X \mid Z). \end{align} \]

Intuitively, the complete description of an instance consists of two parts: i) the “cause” \(Z\) and ii) the “effect” \(X\) that is generated from \(Z\). The complete (joint) probability of the instance is then given by the product of the two parts:

\[ \begin{align} p(X, Z) = p(X \mid Z)\, p(Z). \label{eq:joint} \end{align} \]

This belief CANNOT be verified directly. Despite the misleading name (with a “complete”), at this stage, the job of designing a *learning system* is *incomplete* in the sense what we compute (\(p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})\)) cannot be compared with what we can observe (samples of \(X\): \(\{\mathbf{x}_{1}, \mathbf{x}_{2}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{N}\}\)), as the corresponding \(Z: \{\mathbf{z}_{1}, \mathbf{z}_{2}, \dots, \mathbf{z}_{N}\}\) are never observed.

To proceed, we must carry out a marginalisation step over the latent variable \(Z\) to recover the distribution over the observed variable \(X\). The term marginalisation means to aggregate all the possible values of \(Z\) that could have generated \(X=\mathbf{x}\), weighted by their probabilities.

\[ p(X) = \int p(X \mid Z)\, p(Z)\, \mathrm{d}Z. \]

This marginal likelihood \(p(X)\) is the quantity that we can compare with the observed data samples, where the comparison is used to adjust the model and learning is achieved.

At this point, it is natural to ask: why bother? We introduce an unobservable latent variable \(Z\), posit a joint distribution \(p(X, Z)\) that can never be verified directly against the data, and then expend considerable effort to marginalise out \(Z\) in order to recover the observable \(p(X)\) — only to proceed with learning as before.

The answer is that latent-variable models do *not* introduce new complexities in the model; they serve as vehicles of mathematical construction that allow to *expressively discuss* the complexity. Specifically, we *decompose* the modelling: the single hard task of directly modelling a complex high-dimensional \(p(X)\) is broken into several simpler, individually tractable components that can be specified, learnt, and reasoned about separately.

There are three components in the standard latent-variable model:

- 

**The prior** \(p(Z)\): usually a simple, low-dimensional distribution such as an isotropic Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_K)\). The low dimensionality (\(K \ll d\)) encodes the inductive bias that the essential covariance structure of the data can be captured with only a few degrees of freedom. The simplicity of the prior encodes the absence of any specific prior belief about the geometry of that structure; learning is left to discover it.

- 

**The structural map** \(f_\theta: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}\): a (to-be) learned function that maps each latent code \(\mathbf{z}\) to the *expected* value of the corresponding observation in data space. This is where the expressive power of the model resides: \(f_\theta\) must learn the low-dimensional manifold on which the data concentrate.

- 

**The observation noise** \(p(X \mid f_\theta(Z))\): a structureless distribution in the high-dimensional data space — typically an isotropic Gaussian with mean \(f_\theta(\mathbf{z})\) and a small variance. It accounts for the residual variation in \(X\) that is not explained by the latent code, making the model’s constraint on the data *soft* rather than exact.

In this formulation, \(Z\) represents the essential, low-dimensional information about the data. The low dimension of \(Z\) is an inductive bias: it restricts the model to seek explanations that lie on a low-dimensional manifold, which is both a realistic assumption for structured data and a necessary regulariser for learning to generalise. The observation noise makes this constraint *soft*: residual variations that cannot be fully accounted for by the latent code are absorbed by \(p(X \mid f_\theta(Z))\), so the model need not force every data point onto the manifold exactly. This *division of labour* — structural modelling in the latent space, uncertainty modelling in the observation noise — is what makes the latent-variable framework both tractable and expressive.

### Learning Latent-Variable Models and an Equivalence Class of Hard Problems

The prior \(p(\mathbf{z})\) and the noise model \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid f_\theta(\mathbf{z}))\) are usually fixed to simple forms, so the main modelling challenge is to *learn* the structural map \(f_\theta : \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}\) from data. Learning \(f_\theta\) requires the **Evaluation Quest** as a prerequisite. Here is why: the core logic is to establish, for a given candidate \(\theta\), how the resulting model assigns probabilities to observations — i.e.\ to make \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)\) computable. Once this dependency is in place, one can evaluate \(p_\theta\) on the observed data \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}\) to obtain the in-sample criterion \(E_\mathrm{in}(\theta)\), and then use its gradient to update \(\theta\).

Concretely, learning is achieved by maximising the marginal log-likelihood:

\[ \begin{align} \log p(\{\mathbf{x}_i\} \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \int p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \mathbf{z})\, p(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}, \label{eq:marginalisation} \end{align} \]

where the marginalisation over \(\mathbf{z}\) recovers the observable likelihood from the joint model. Each summand is the logarithm of a high-dimensional integral over \(\mathcal{Z}\), with \(\theta\) coupled to the integrand through \(f_\theta\) inside \(p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \mathbf{z})\). Taking the logarithm converts the product to a sum but does *not* simplify the integral: each term remains a \(\log\) of an intractable integral, for which no closed form exists in the nonlinear case. The **Evaluation Quest** is therefore the central bottleneck of learning.

**Remark: Marginalization by inference.** The intractability of likelihood evaluation is often resolved in practice by solving the related problem of *inference* — computing the posterior \(p(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})\), or just take a sample \(\mathbf{z}^* \sim p(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})\) from it.

The posterior is highly concentrated around the latent code \(\mathbf{z}^*\) that best explains \(\mathbf{x}\), so it is a good proxy for the integral over \(\mathbf{z}\). Concretely, Bayes’ rule can be rearranged as

\[ \begin{align} p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}^*)\,p(\mathbf{z}^*)}{p(\mathbf{z}^*\mid\mathbf{x})}, \label{eq:bayes_rearranged} \end{align} \]

which holds exactly for any choice of \(\mathbf{z}^*\). Given a sample \(\mathbf{z}^*\!\sim p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\), the numerator is immediately evaluable: \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}^*)\) is the decoder likelihood and \(p(\mathbf{z}^*)\) is the prior, both of which are simple, explicit distributions. The denominator \(p(\mathbf{z}^*\mid\mathbf{x})\) is the posterior density at \(\mathbf{z}^*\). Because the posterior is concentrated, \(\mathbf{z}^*\) carries virtually all of its mass; with several such samples one can estimate this density reliably by standard methods, and in the extreme of a sharply peaked posterior a single sample already makes the formula above a good approximation. This is the key insight: *access to the posterior* (task (ii)) is what unlocks the evaluation of \(p(\mathbf{x})\) (task (i)).

Once \(f_\theta\) has been learned, **Sampling** finally becomes trivial: draw \(\mathbf{z} \sim p(\mathbf{z})\) and then \(\mathbf{x} \sim p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\) — this is ancestral sampling and requires no integration at all. Note that this ease of sampling is entirely *consequent* on having solved the hard Evaluation problem first; it is not an independent capability obtained for free.

## Classical Baselines: Naïve Bayes and Probabilistic PCA

Before turning to deep generative models, it is instructive to look at two classical instances that already embody the core ideas.

### Naïve Bayes.

The simplest tractable model of a joint distribution over a high-dimensional \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)\) is to assume that all components are *conditionally independent* given the class label \(y\):

\[ p(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} p(x_j \mid y). \]

This is the Naïve Bayes assumption—“naïve” because pixel values, or word occurrences, are rarely truly independent. Despite its strong assumption, Naïve Bayes is surprisingly effective for text classification and yields a closed-form, computationally trivial model. Its principal limitation is exactly its simplicity: it cannot capture correlations among features, and will therefore miss the low-dimensional manifold structure that characterises real data.

### Probabilistic PCA.

Probabilistic PCA (PPCA; Tipping and Bishop, 1999) re-interprets the PCA compression we studied in the previous section as a latent-variable model. The generative story is:

\[ \begin{align} \mathbf{z} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{I}_K), \label{eq:ppca_prior} \\ \mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z} &\sim \mathcal{N}(W\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu},\, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_d), \label{eq:ppca_likelihood} \end{align} \]

where \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times K}\) is the *loading matrix*, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) is the data mean, and \(\sigma^2\) controls the noise level. The latent variable \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^K\) plays the role of the PCA coordinates, and the model assumes that, given \(\mathbf{z}\), the observed \(\mathbf{x}\) is a noisy linear transformation of \(\mathbf{z}\). PPCA is a fully probabilistic model: it supports both likelihood evaluation \(\log p(\mathbf{x})\) (the marginal obtained by integrating out \(\mathbf{z}\) is Gaussian and closed-form) and sampling. It also provides a principled treatment of missing data and model comparison via marginal likelihood.

The limitation is the same as plain PCA: the mapping from \(\mathbf{z}\) to \(\mathbf{x}\) is *linear*. The path forward is to replace \(W\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu}\) with a nonlinear neural network, while retaining the latent-variable structure—which leads directly to the Variational Autoencoder.

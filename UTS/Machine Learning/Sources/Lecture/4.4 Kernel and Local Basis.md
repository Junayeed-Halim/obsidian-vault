# Model Design II: Local Basis Functions and Kernel Methods

In the previous section, we built a hypothesis space by composing *global* basis functions—functions that are non-zero almost everywhere and encode structure through their overall shape. A neural network is the canonical instance: each hidden unit responds to every input, and the network’s expressivity comes from combining many such globally-defined patterns.

This section explores a different philosophy. Instead of asking “what global shapes capture the data?”, one can ask “how to propagate the observed answers from one location in the data space to another, based on similarity?” This shift in perspective leads naturally to locally supported basis functions, and, when taken to its logical conclusion, to Kernel Methods.

Kernel methods follow the philosophy of *local* basis functions, but with a crucial twist: instead of explicitly partitioning the space \(\mathcal{X}\) into regions (like a Decision Tree), Kernel Methods implicitly define \(\mathcal{H}\) based on the *similarity* between data points.

## The Core Idea: Similarity-Driven Extrapolation

Recall the “discrete design” we had when starting to design a function space. We randomly split the data space \(\mathcal{X}\) into a certain number of regions, say 100 or 1,000, and the basis functions take the form of indicator functions that are 1 inside a region and 0 elsewhere.

The design is obviously suboptimal: the partitioning is done by randomly “seeding” centres across the space. As the surrounding region of each centre takes a constant output value, and the whole purpose of learning is to generalise the target function *from the observed values at training samples*—why not place the basis-function centres *at the training points themselves*, and let each basis respond to how close a query point is to the training point it is anchored at?

Left: A random partitioning of the data space \(\mathcal{X}\) into 100 regions. Right: A partitioning where each training point is a centre. The basis functions are now “hulls” anchored at the training points, and their shape is determined by the similarity metric.

The two figures illustrate the contrast between random seeding and training-point seeding as region centres. A striking feature of the right figure is the highly irregular cell shapes: because the training data is not uniformly distributed, the Voronoi cells are much finer in the dense regions and much coarser where data is sparse. Far from being a flaw, this irregularity is actually desirable—it is the partition adapting itself to the data distribution.

This observation points to a general principle. Our confidence in the function approximation should diminish as we move away from the training data: near a training point we have direct evidence; far from all training points we are essentially extrapolating into the unknown. In high dimensions, where the training data occupies an exponentially small fraction of the ambient space, this consideration becomes not merely convenient but essential.

Technically, the training-sample-based partition produces a hypothesis space whose local basis functions are the indicator functions of the Voronoi cells of the training points—this is just to say that a query point \(\mathbf{x}\) is assigned the target value of its nearest training point. This is precisely the 1-nearest-neighbour (1-NN) model—a well-known non-parametric method that, in this light, is simply the most rudimentary instance of data-anchored local basis functions.

The hard indicator basis, however, encodes proximity in the crudest possible way: a point just inside a Voronoi cell elicits the same response as the training point at its centre, while a point just outside elicits none at all, regardless of how close it actually is. The natural correction is to replace the binary switch with a *continuous* response that varies smoothly with distance—large near the anchor point and diminishing as the query point moves away. This leads to a much more flexible hypothesis space and a much more powerful model—the Kernel Method.

## Kernels as Smooth Local Basis Functions

When the indicator functions of the Voronoi cells are replaced by smooth bumps, the resulting hypothesis space becomes (roughly)

\[ h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \cdot \bigl[\text{how much } \mathbf{x}_i \text{ influences } \mathbf{x}\bigr] \]

The principal method for quantifying how much one point influences another is to define a symmetric function that accepts two inputs and outputs a single number measuring their relationship. Formally, a kernel function is a symmetric, positive-semi-definite function \(k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\):

\[ k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = k(\mathbf{x}', \mathbf{x}), \qquad \sum_{i,j} c_i c_j\, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \geq 0 \quad \forall\, \{c_i\} \subset \mathbb{R},\; \{\mathbf{x}_i\} \subset \mathcal{X}. \]

These conditions ensure that the kernel induces a geometrically coherent function space: symmetry guarantees that similarity is mutual, while positive semi-definiteness ensures the induced space admits a valid inner product and a well-defined notion of distance. Formally, every such kernel generates a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). You can forgo the technical details on a first reading; we focus on the intuition here rather than the full functional-analytic machinery.

With a kernel function \(k(\cdot, \cdot)\) in hand, the “local blobs” centred at each training point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) can be simply specified as \(k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot)\)—binding one input of the kernel function to the training point \(\mathbf{x}_i\), taking any input \(\mathbf{x}\) and outputting how similar it is to \(\mathbf{x}_i\).

The first five panes show the local basis functions \(k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot)\) induced by the kernel, each anchored at a different training point. The final pane shows the overall function obtained by linearly combining these local bases with the training labels as coefficients.

The figure above demonstrates this construction concretely using the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel:

\[ k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \]

where \(\sigma\) is a bandwidth parameter that governs the spatial reach of each basis function. A smaller \(\sigma\) produces narrow, sharply localised bumps; a larger \(\sigma\) yields broad, heavily overlapping ones. In either case the kernel evaluates to \(1\) when \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}'\) and decays smoothly to zero as the two points diverge, so the influence of every training point is strictly local and commensurate with proximity. The resulting model prediction is then a smooth, data-adaptive interpolant—each training label \(y_i\) contributes to a query point \(\mathbf{x}\) in direct proportion to \(k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x})\).

The hypothesis space based on kernels. We can now state the kernel hypothesis space more precisely. Given \(N\) training points \(\{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_N\}\) and a kernel \(k\), each \(k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot)\) is a data-anchored basis function. The hypothesis space is taken to be their linear span:

\[ \mathcal{H} = \operatorname{span}\bigl\{k(\mathbf{x}_1, \cdot),\, k(\mathbf{x}_2, \cdot),\, \ldots,\, k(\mathbf{x}_N, \cdot)\bigr\}, \]

so every candidate function \(h \in \mathcal{H}\) takes the form

\[ h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathbb{R}^N. \]

This is a clean, finite-dimensional representation: we have traded in the uncountable freedom of an arbitrary function for \(N\) real coefficients \(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_N\), one per training point.

### Common Kernel Functions

The choice of the kernel function \(k\) defines the shape of our local basis functions, and thus defines the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\). We have seen the Gaussian RBF kernel in the motivating example, but there are many others, each encoding different assumptions about the data and the function we wish to learn.

- **Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel:** \[\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\] It evaluates to \(1\) when \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}'\) and decays exponentially as the points move apart. The parameter \(\sigma\) controls the “width” of the local bump.
- **Polynomial Kernel:** \[\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}' + c)^d\] This kernel implicitly maps the data into a high-dimensional space of polynomial features (like our XOR example) without ever explicitly computing those features.
- **Linear Kernel:** \[\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}'\] The simplest kernel, corresponding to the standard inner product. It does not induce any nonlinearity, so the resulting hypothesis space is just the space of linear functions.

Representative algorithms that use this design pattern include Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Gaussian Processes (GPs).

### Advantages of Kernel Methods

- **Adaptive Capacity.** Unlike a neural network with a fixed architecture, the number of basis functions in a kernel method grows automatically with the size of the training data \(N\). More data means more local bases, naturally increasing the expressivity of \(\mathcal{H}\).
- **No Explicit Partitioning.** Unlike Decision Trees, we do not need to manually or algorithmically slice the space \(\mathcal{X}\) into discrete boxes. We only need to define a sensible similarity metric.

### A Word of Caution

The picture above is deliberately informal. Two technical points are glossed over here:

- **Normalisation.** The raw sum \(\sum_i \alpha_i k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x})\) is not a proper weighted average: the kernel values do not sum to one. Practical estimators commonly require normalisation to ensure the output is on the same scale as the training labels: \[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_i y_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})}{\displaystyle\sum_j k(\mathbf{x}_j,\mathbf{x})}.\]
- **Determining \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).** Setting \(\alpha_i = y_i\) (using the training labels directly as coefficients) is natural and intuitive but generally leads to technical difficulties in analysing the resulting function’s properties. Practical algorithms also often require regularisation to prevent overfitting, which further complicates the relationship between \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) and the training labels. The coefficients are typically *learned* by minimising a regularised objective over the training data—this is what kernel ridge regression, support vector machines, and Gaussian process inference all do, each in a slightly different way.

These details are addressed in optional topics that unify kernel methods and linear models. The essential structural message is this: a kernel function provides an \(N\)-dimensional hypothesis space that is anchored to the data, adaptive in resolution, and free of any explicit spatial partitioning.

## Historical Prototypes ★

This idea of using a “kernel” to extrapolate from known information to unknown regions is not a modern machine learning invention; it has deep roots in classical mathematics.

- **Cauchy Integral Formula (Complex Analysis):** If a function \(f\) is analytic inside a region \(D\), its value at any interior point \(z_0\) is completely determined by its values on the boundary \(\partial D\): \[f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\partial D} \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z_0}\, d\zeta.\] Here, the known information is on the boundary, and the kernel \(\tfrac{1}{\zeta - z_0}\) reconstructs the interior.
- **Poisson Kernel (Heat Equation):** If we know the temperature distribution \(f(1, \phi)\) on the boundary of a disk, the temperature at any interior point \((r, \theta)\) is given by: \[f(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} P_r(\theta - \phi)\, f(1, \phi)\, d\phi.\] The Poisson kernel \(P_r\) acts as the weighting function that extrapolates the boundary data inwards.

The underlying philosophy is identical: we exploit the “regularity” or “smoothness” of the space to infer unknown values from known ones.

## A Unified Perspective: Global vs. Local Basis ★

We have presented Perceptrons and Neural Networks (global basis) and Kernel Methods (local basis) as two distinct design patterns. They seem to be on two extremes of the spectrum of how we construct the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\). Surprisingly, they are not as different as they appear. Deep theoretical results show that these two approaches unify, at least in certain *limits*.

### Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces: Kernels as Infinite-Dimensional Global Bases

The connection between the “local basis” view and the classical linear-model view becomes clear when we interpret each training point not by its coordinates in \(\mathcal{X}\), but by its *relationship* to every other point—that is, by the function \(k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot)\). This map

\[ \phi:\; \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}, \qquad \phi(\mathbf{x}) = k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot), \]

embeds every input into the RKHS \(\mathcal{H}\) and preserves all pairwise similarity information: \(\langle \phi(\mathbf{x}), \phi(\mathbf{x}') \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\). The inner product in \(\mathcal{H}\) is defined so that the kernel function itself serves as the inner product of the embeddings:

\[ \langle k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot), k(\mathbf{x}', \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'). \]

The central identity of RKHS theory is the reproducing property:

\[ \langle f,\; k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = f(\mathbf{x}) \qquad \forall\, f \in \mathcal{H},\; \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}. \]

Evaluating a function at a point is the same as taking an inner product with the kernel centred at that point.

In this lifted space, the model \(h\) is simply a *linear* function \(h(\mathbf{x}) = \langle \mathbf{w}, k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\). However, for many kernels (e.g. the RBF kernel), \(\mathcal{H}\) is infinite-dimensional and \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{H}\)—the weight vector is itself a function in \(\mathcal{H}\), and it is impossible to represent, solve for, or even utilise \(\mathbf{w}\) directly.

Fortunately, we can bypass this issue by leveraging two key facts:

- **Interpolation conditions.** If we require \(h(\mathbf{x}_i) = y_i\), then by the reproducing property this is exactly \(\langle \mathbf{w}, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = y_i\)—a linear constraint on \(\mathbf{w}\) in \(\mathcal{H}\).
- **Implicit prediction.** A prediction at any test point \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(h(\mathbf{x}) = \langle \mathbf{w}, k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\), so we never need to materialise \(\mathbf{w}\) explicitly—we only need the ability to compute inner products \(\langle \mathbf{w}, k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\).

And we also have the Representer Theorem:

For any regularised empirical risk minimisation problem in an RKHS, the minimiser \(\mathbf{w}^*\) lies in the finite-dimensional subspace spanned by the training embeddings \(\{k(\mathbf{x}_1, \cdot), \ldots, k(\mathbf{x}_N, \cdot)\}\).

That is, \(\mathbf{w}^* = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \cdot)\) for some \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathbb{R}^N\), and therefore

\[ h(\mathbf{x}) = \langle \mathbf{w}^*, k(\mathbf{x}, \cdot) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}). \]

The infinite-dimensional optimisation problem collapses to solving for \(N\) real coefficients—exactly the finite sum we arrived at from the local-basis intuition, now derived rigorously. This is the kernel trick in its full generality: a nonlinear model in \(\mathcal{X}\) is equivalent to a linear model in the (potentially infinite-dimensional) feature space \(\mathcal{H}\).

### Infinite-Width Neural Networks and Gaussian Processes

The RKHS perspective already hinted that kernel methods are, at heart, linear models in a (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space. The converse direction is equally striking: neural networks, taken to the limit of infinite width, *become* kernel methods.

Infinite width → Gaussian Process. Consider a single-layer network with \(m\) hidden units, each computing a nonlinear basis function \(g_i(\mathbf{x})\) with output weights \(v_i\) drawn i.i.d. at initialisation:

\[ f_m(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{i=1}^m v_i\, g_i(\mathbf{x}). \]

As \(m \to \infty\), a central-limit-theorem argument shows that \(f_m\) converges in distribution to a Gaussian Process:

\[ f_m(\mathbf{x}) \;\xrightarrow{m \to \infty}\; \mathcal{GP}\!\bigl(0,\; k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\bigr), \]

where the covariance kernel \(k\) is determined entirely by the choice of activation function and weight distribution.

A Gaussian Process (GP) is a probability distribution over *functions*: instead of placing a distribution over a finite-dimensional parameter vector, a GP specifies, for any finite collection of inputs \(\{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n\}\), a joint Gaussian distribution over the corresponding function values \(\{f(\mathbf{x}_1), \ldots, f(\mathbf{x}_n)\}\). The entire distribution is characterised by two objects: a mean function \(\mu(\mathbf{x})\) and a *covariance function* (kernel) \(k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\), which encodes how strongly the function values at two inputs co-vary. A kernel method with kernel \(k\) defines a deterministic hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\); the corresponding GP with the same \(k\) places a probability measure over the *same* space of functions. In this sense, every kernel defines both a hypothesis space and a natural prior distribution over it—the two are two sides of the same coin.

The result above is therefore a statement about the *function space* induced by a randomly initialised network, not about any particular trained model. At random initialisation, before any gradient step is taken, the distribution over functions that the network can represent—as the output weights vary over their random draws—is exactly a GP with a kernel determined by the architecture. In other words, the hypothesis space carved out by a random single-layer network of infinite width is not merely *analogous* to a kernel method’s hypothesis space—it *coincides* with it, and the kernel is dictated entirely by the activation function and the weight prior.

Finite width: the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). A more recent result extends this connection to networks of finite width. Jacot et al. (2018) showed that, for a sufficiently wide network trained by gradient descent, the function learned at convergence is well-approximated by kernel regression with a specific, architecture-determined kernel—the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). Concretely, the training dynamics of the network remain close to those of a linear model in the NTK feature space throughout training, provided the network is wide enough that the kernel changes negligibly as the weights are updated.

Synthesis. These two results—the infinite-width GP limit and the NTK—show that the apparent dichotomy between global (neural network) and local (kernel) basis functions is not fundamental. Both design strategies are instances of a single underlying question:

How should we distribute degrees of freedom across the continuous space \(\mathcal{X}\) so as to best approximate the target function from a finite sample?

Neural networks answer this question by composing parametric, globally-defined transformations and optimising over their parameters. Kernel methods answer it by anchoring one basis function at each training point and letting the geometry of the kernel encode all prior assumptions about smoothness. The limit theorems above demonstrate that these two philosophies converge—literally—when the neural network is made wide enough, and that the language of kernels and RKHS provides the unifying mathematical framework for both.

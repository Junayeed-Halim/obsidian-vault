## How do we compare Hypotheses?

We have our data \(S\) and our candidates \(\mathcal{H}\). Now, how do we choose the *best* candidate? We need a measuring stick. This is the **Criterion**, often formulated as a **Loss Function**.

## Point-wise Loss

First, we define how "bad" a prediction is for a single example. A loss function \(\ell(h(x), y)\) quantifies the penalty for predicting \(h(x)\) when the true label is \(y\).

- **0-1 Loss (Classification):** \(\ell(h(x), y) = 1\) if \(h(x) \neq y\), else \(0\). Measures raw error rate.
- **Squared Loss (Regression):** \(\ell(h(x), y) = (h(x) - y)^2\). Penalizes large errors heavily.

## Risk: The Aggregate Score

We want a hypothesis that works well *overall*. We transform the point-wise loss into a global score called **Risk**.

### The True Risk (The Ideal Goal)

Ideally, we want to minimize the expected loss over the *entire* universe of data (the distribution \(P\)). This is called the **True Risk** or **Generalization Error**:

\[ R(h) = E_{(x,y) \sim P} [\ell(h(x), y)] \]

If we could calculate this, we would find the perfect model that works everywhere, distinct from just memorizing the past. But we cannot compute this because we don't know \(P\).

### The Empirical Risk (The Practical Proxy)

Since we only have the training set \(S\), we approximate the True Risk with the average loss on our observable data. This is the **Empirical Risk** or **Training Error**:

\[ \hat{R}_S(h) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \ell(h(x_i), y_i) \]

## Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)

This leads to the central dogma of modern Machine Learning, the principle of **Empirical Risk Minimization**: To find the best function, we look for the hypothesis \(h^* \in \mathcal{H}\) that minimizes the Empirical Risk:

\[ h^* = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \hat{R}_S(h) \]

We hope that if \(\mathcal{H}\) is chosen carefully (Inductive Bias) and the number of samples \(m\) is large enough, minimizing the Empirical Risk \(\hat{R}_S(h)\) will also minimize the True Risk \(R(h)\). This hope is the heart of **Generalization**.

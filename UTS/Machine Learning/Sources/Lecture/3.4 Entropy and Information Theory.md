# Information-Theoretic Foundations

Although the intuitive explanation above is sufficient to guide engineering implementations, to profoundly understand why this specific mathematical form is chosen, we must introduce the fundamental concepts of Information Theory.

Put it simply, we will check out a few first principles regarding the measurement of uncertainty and the change of it. "Changing (reducing) the amount of uncertainty" is taken as "providing information", which fundamentally defines tasks for intelligent systems -- after all, given input \(X\), all we can obtain from an intelligent system is the information about the target \(Y\) of our interest.

The term "Entropy", which appears in our loss function above, is a measure unit of uncertainty and therefore "information". One should have encountered this term in the previous subjects when the specific model "ID3 Decision Trees" was introduced.

Recall the definition: Let \(X\) be a discrete random variable with possible values \(\{x_1, x_2, ..., x_n\}\) and probability mass function \(P(X)\), \(\textrm{Prob}(\{X=x_i\}) := P(x_i)\), the Entropy of \(X\) is defined as

\[ H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i) \log P(x_i) \]

Some comments on the denotations are in order. First, the input argument of the entropy \(H\), conventionally denoted as \(X\), carries the meaning of a random variable AND the distribution of it. It could be denoted as fully as

\begin{align*} &H\bigg( \Big[ \textrm{Prob}(X=x_1), \textrm{Prob}(X=x_2), ..., \textrm{Prob}(X=x_n) \Big] \bigg) \\ &=H\bigg( \Big[ P(x_1), P(x_2), ..., P(x_n) \Big] \bigg) \end{align*}

which explicitly lists the quantities used in the computaton on the right-hand side of the definition. But \(H(X)\) is more concise and becomes popular and standard. Second, in all discussions in this section, the latin letter \(X\), \(Y\) etc. has nothing to do with the meaning of the "observation" or "target" in learning/data analytics context, as we have been using them so far. They are just random variables in the mathematical sense. In what follows, it is totally possible to treat \(X\) as the true distribution of the labels and \(Y\) as the predicted distribution of the labels. Also note that two *different* random variables can share the *same* set of outcomes. It is even possible that two *different* random variables share both the same set of outcomes and the same probability distribution over them -- imagine throwing two identical dices and recording the outcome of each dice respectively.

Now we can review our study of the notion entropy and how it materialises the idea of measuring uncertainty. ## Entropy as a measure of uncertainty

First, we must keep in mind that the idea of "uncertainty" is associated with a random *system* -- there has to be *multiple* possible outcomes for the system to be uncertain about. Now we start with the simplest possible situation of such systems, namely, all possible outcomes are *equally likely*. Now instead of writing the formula in full, \(H(X) = H([\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}, ..., \frac{1}{n}])\), we will use simplified symbol and write it as \(A(n)\), which is a function of an integer \(n\) only.

We will derive the definition of entropy from a few reasonable assumptions in this case.

- \(A(n)\) should be monotonically increasing with \(n\). I.e. for equally-likely systems, the more possible outcomes there are, the more uncertain the system is.
- \(A(n)\) should be *additive*. That is, if we have two independent systems with \(n\) and \(m\) possible outcomes respectively. Now consider a *combined* of the two, which has \(n \times m\) equally possible outcomes, e.g. with two factors about a play card, one is the suit (\(4\times\): hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades) and the other is the rank (\(13\times\): A, 2, ..., K), there are \(4\times 13=52\) possible outcomes for the combined system. Then the combined system's uncertainty should be the *sum* of the uncertainties of the two individual systems. I.e. \(A(\textrm{Play Card}) = A(\textrm{Suit}) + A(\textrm{Rank})\), \(A(52) = A(4) + A(13)\).

Now consider two systems with \(n\) and \(m\) possible outcomes respectively. Without loss of generality, we can assume that \(m \[ l \times A(m) = A(m^l) \le A(n^k) = k \times A(n) 

The inequality above is via the monotonicity property of \(A(\cdot)\). Now we can divide the whole inequality by \(k\) and by \(A(m)\), and we have

\[ \frac{l}{k} \le \frac{A(n)}{A(m)} \leq \frac{l+1}{k} \]

This inequality holds for any \(k\) and the corresponding \(l\). We will take \(k\) to infinity and in the limit \(k\to \infty\), the difference between \(\frac{l+1}{k}\) and \(\frac{l}{k}\) will vanish, and we have

\[ \frac{A(n)}{A(m)} = \lim_{k\to \infty} \frac{l(k)}{k} \]

Note that \(l\) depends on \(k\) (we have found a number \(l\) AFTER we have fixed \(k\)), so we write it as \(l(k)\) to make the dependence explicit. Next we will find the relationship between \(l\) and \(k\), and its value in the limit \(k\to \infty\).

Recall that \(l\) is defined as the integer such that \(m^l \le n^k \leq m^{l+1}\), which is

\begin{align} l \log m \leq k \log n \leq (l+1) \log m \nonumber \\ \frac{l}{k} \leq \frac{\log n}{\log m} \leq \frac{l+1}{k} \end{align}

Now we can take the limit \(k\to \infty\) and we have

\[ \frac{A(n)}{A(m)} = \lim_{k\to \infty} \frac{l(k)}{k} = \frac{\log n}{\log m} \]

So we have \(A(n) = C \log n\) for some constant \(C\). Note it does not matter which base of the logarithm we use, as changing the base will only change the value by a constant factor, which can be absorbed into the definition of \(C\). Actually, we are free to choose the base of the logarithm, and the choice will only change the unit of the entropy, not unlike one can speak of distance in terms of miles or kilometers. Two common choices are letting \(C=1\) and the base of the logarithm as \(2\) or \(e\), corresponding to units of bits and nats, respectively.

### Intuitions about the entropy of equally-likely systems

A simple intuition that may help understand the motivation leading to the choice of the logarithm form of the measure of uncertainty is as follows.

If we think of the measure of uncertainty as "the length of a message that can uniquely identify the outcome of the system", then with an alphabet of \(b\) symbols, with every one more symbol in the message, we can distinguish \(b\) times more outcomes. This is independent of how we decide to encode the message, as long as we want to be as efficient as possible (i.e. each bit worth its cost of communication). Then with a message of length \(n\), we can uniquely describe the outcome of a system with \(b^n\) possible outcomes, which is equivalent to the derivation above.

## Entropy of general uncertain systems

Now we have derived the form of the entropy for the special case of equally-likely systems. The next natural step is to ask, how about if my system has \(n\) possible outcomes with probabilities \(p_1, p_2, ..., p_n\) that are not necessarily equal?

Again we treat a simplified case first, where the probabilities are rational numbers, i.e. \(p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}\) for some integers \(n_i\) and \(N\). In most interesting cases, this means we have "splitted" the original \(n\)-outcome system into a larger system with \(N\) equally-likely outcomes, where \(N \gg n\). E.g. \(n=3, p_1=0.24, p_2=0.25, p_3=0.51\), we can split the system into \(N=100\) equally-likely outcomes, where \(n_1=24\) of them correspond to the first outcome, \(n_2=25\) of them correspond to the second outcome, and \(n_3=51\) of them correspond to the third outcome.

Then we appeal to the additivity property of the measure of uncertainty, and we have

\[ \underbrace{H(X)}_{\text{entropy of interest}} + \underbrace{p_1 \cdot A(n_1) + p_2 \cdot A(n_2) + \dots + p_n \cdot A(n_n)}_{\text{entropy of sub-systems}} = A(N) \]

where the right-hand side is the size of a message to specify within the larger system of \(N\) equally-likely outcomes; and the left-hand side just states that the specification could be acheieved by two steps: i) specify the outcome of one of the \(n\) outcomes of the system of interest (probability \(p_i\) are different for different outcomes) and ii) specify the outcome within the sub-system of \(n_i\) equally-likely outcomes (the average size of the message for this step is the weighted average of \(A(n_i)\), where the weights are \(p_i\)). Substituting \(A(n) = \log n\) into the equation above, we have

\[ H(X) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log n_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log N \]

Note on the right, the \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log N\) is just \(\log N\) because \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1\). Then we have

\[ H(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log N - \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log n_i = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log \frac{n_i}{N} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log p_i \]

which is the definition of the entropy for a general system with \(n\) possible outcomes and probabilities \(p_1, p_2, ..., p_n\).

Finally, for the case where the probabilities are irrational numbers, we can approximate them with rational numbers and take the limit, and we will have the same formula for the entropy.

The definition of entropy for a more general system with continuous outcomes is more technical and we will not cover it here. With the tool at hand, we are ready to study the information-theoretic foundation of the loss function we have introduced in the previous section.

## KL Divergence: comparing two distributions

We now have a rigorous measure of the uncertainty of a single distribution. The natural next question is: given two distributions over the same set of outcomes, how do we measure the *discrepancy* between them? This is the question that KL divergence (Kullback--Leibler divergence) answers.

Again, let us build up the concept from first principles rather than simply stating the formula. Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be two probability distributions over the same finite set of outcomes \(\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}\). Think of \(P\) as the *true* distribution -- the one that actually generates outcomes -- and \(Q\) as a *proposed* or *approximate* distribution, which is what we believe or model the world to be.

Now borrow the message-length interpretation of entropy from above. If outcomes are generated by \(P\), but we design our communication code assuming they come from \(Q\), what is the average message length we end up using? Under the optimal code designed for \(Q\), the code word for outcome \(x_i\) is assigned a length of \(-\log Q(x_i)\) bits (this is the definition of the optimal code for a distribution -- shorter codes for more probable outcomes). However, since outcomes are *actually* drawn from \(P\), the true average message length is

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[-\log Q(x)\right] = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i) \log Q(x_i). \]

Compare this to the length we would have used if we had designed the code *correctly* for \(P\), which is the entropy \(H(P) = -\sum_i P(x_i) \log P(x_i)\). The *extra* message length incurred by the mismatch between \(P\) and \(Q\) is

\begin{align} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) &:= \left(-\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log Q(x_i)\right) - \left(-\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log P(x_i)\right) \nonumber \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log \frac{P(x_i)}{Q(x_i)}. \end{align}

In practice, when the label of a sample is observed to be \(x_i\), it can be considered that the true distribution \(P\) is a one-hot distribution that puts all its mass on \(x_i\): \(P(x_i) = 1\) and \(P(x_j) = 0\) for \(j \neq i\). Then the KL divergence from \(P\) to \(Q\) is simply \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) = \log \frac{1}{Q(x_i)}\).

There are a few useful properties of KL divergence that are worth noting. But you can skip them if you are not interested in the mathematical details.

Non-negativity.

\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) \ge 0\), with equality if and only if \(P = Q\) everywhere. This follows from *Jensen's inequality*, which states that for a convex function \(f\) and a random variable \(X\),

\[ f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \leq \mathbb{E}[f(X)]. \]

The proof is a clever application of Jensen's inequality to the convex function \(f(x) = -\log x\), and constructing a random variable \(X = \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}\) where \(x\) is drawn from \(P\). Now it becomes

\begin{align} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}\right] \\ &\ge -\log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}\right] = -\log 1 = 0. \nonumber \end{align}

This is reassuring: the extra cost of using a wrong code is never negative.

Asymmetry.

In general, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \| P)\). The two directions have different interpretations. \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)\) penalises regions where \(P\) places mass but \(Q\) does not (because \(\log \frac{P}{Q} \to +\infty\) as \(Q \to 0\) while \(P > 0\)). \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \| P)\) penalises the opposite. In the machine learning context we will almost always use \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)\), where \(P\) is the true distribution and \(Q\) is the model's prediction.

Not a metric.

Because of the asymmetry (and because the triangle inequality does not hold), KL divergence is *not* a distance in the formal sense. Nevertheless, it is the most fundamental and widely used measure of discrepancy between distributions in statistics, information theory, and machine learning.

## Cross-Entropy: KL divergence in disguise

We are now in a position to connect everything back to the cross-entropy loss function introduced in the previous section.

Recall the cross-entropy between \(P\) and \(Q\):

\[ H(P, Q) := -\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log Q(x_i). \]

Rewrite this by adding and subtracting \(H(P)\):

\begin{align} H(P, Q) &= -\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log Q(x_i) \nonumber \\ &= -\sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log P(x_i) + \sum_{i=1}^n P(x_i) \log \frac{P(x_i)}{Q(x_i)} \nonumber \\ &= H(P) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q). \label{eq:ce_decomposition} \end{align}

This identity is the key result of this section. It tells us that the cross-entropy between the true distribution \(P\) and the model distribution \(Q\) decomposes into two terms:

- \(H(P)\): the entropy of the true distribution. This is a property of the data alone, completely independent of the model. No matter how good the model becomes, this term cannot be reduced.
- \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)\): the KL divergence from \(P\) to \(Q\). This is the only term that depends on the model's predictions \(Q\).

Therefore, when we *minimise the cross-entropy loss over the model parameters*, we are doing something very precise: since \(H(P)\) is a constant with respect to the model, we are *exactly* minimising \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q)\). In other words, we are finding the model distribution \(Q\) that is as close as possible to the true distribution \(P\), where "closeness" is measured in the information-theoretic sense of KL divergence.

This gives the cross-entropy loss a much stronger justification than mere computational convenience: it is the *natural* loss function when the task is to fit a probability distribution to data.

## Conditional Entropy and Mutual Information

Having established the tools of entropy and KL divergence, we can now take a step back and ask a more fundamental question about the learning task itself: *how much can a model ever hope to learn?*

Recall our notational convention from this section: \(X\) and \(Y\) here are generic random variables, not the input features and labels of the learning problem. However, we will at the end of this subsection make the explicit connection to the learning setting.

Joint and Conditional Entropy.

Given two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with a joint distribution \(P(X, Y)\), the *joint entropy* is the entropy of the combined system:

\[ H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} P(x, y) \log P(x, y). \]

This measures the total uncertainty about the pair \((X, Y)\) together.

The *conditional entropy* of \(Y\) given \(X\) is defined as the expected entropy of \(Y\) after the value of \(X\) has been revealed:

\begin{align} H(Y \mid X) &:= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P(X)}\left[ H(Y \mid X = x) \right] \\ &= -\sum_{x} P(x) \sum_{y} P(y \mid x) \log P(y \mid x). \end{align}

Equivalently, it can be written compactly as

\[ H(Y \mid X) = -\sum_{x, y} P(x, y) \log P(y \mid x). \]

One can verify directly from the definitions that the joint entropy satisfies the *chain rule*:

\[ H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X). \]

This is the formal counterpart of the additivity property we used to derive entropy in the first place: the uncertainty about the pair \((X, Y)\) equals the uncertainty about \(X\) plus the remaining uncertainty about \(Y\) once \(X\) is known.

Non-negativity of Conditional Entropy.

It is always the case that \(H(Y \mid X) \ge 0\), because it is a weighted average of entropies \(H(Y \mid X = x)\), each of which is non-negative. Furthermore, \(H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)\): knowing \(X\) can only reduce (or leave unchanged) our uncertainty about \(Y\), never increase it. Formally:

\[ H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y), \quad \text{with equality iff } X \text{ and } Y \text{ are independent.} \]

Mutual Information.

The reduction in uncertainty about \(Y\) after observing \(X\) is called the *mutual information* between \(X\) and \(Y\):

\[ I(X; Y) := H(Y) - H(Y \mid X). \]

Substituting the chain rule, one can show that mutual information is *symmetric*: \(I(X; Y) = I(Y; X)\). It can also be written as a KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of marginals:

\begin{align} I(X; Y) &= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P(X, Y) \,\Big\|\, P(X) P(Y)\right) \\ &= \sum_{x,y} P(x,y) \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}. \end{align}

This form makes the symmetry and non-negativity of \(I(X;Y)\) immediate from the properties of KL divergence we derived earlier.

Connection to the learning task.

Now we translate back to the language of machine learning. Let us identify \(X\) with the model's input (feature vector) and \(Y\) with the target label. The marginal entropy \(H(Y)\) measures the *a priori* uncertainty about the label before any input is observed. The conditional entropy \(H(Y \mid X)\) measures the *irreducible* uncertainty that remains after the input has been fully observed -- this is the Bayes error, the fundamental noise floor of the task that no model can overcome. The mutual information \(I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\) is therefore the maximum amount of information about \(Y\) that is in principle extractable from \(X\).

When we train a neural network by minimising the cross-entropy loss \(H(P, Q_\theta)\) over parameters \(\theta\), we are -- from the decomposition -- minimising \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P(Y \mid X) \| Q_\theta(Y \mid X))\). The best achievable value of the cross-entropy loss (over all possible models) is

\[ \min_\theta H(P, Q_\theta) = H(Y \mid X), \]

which is exactly the conditional entropy of the labels given the inputs. A model achieves this minimum if and only if its predicted distribution \(Q_\theta(Y \mid X)\) perfectly matches the true conditional \(P(Y \mid X)\).

In summary: the entire learning process of fitting a classifier by minimising cross-entropy can be interpreted as the attempt to *capture as much of the mutual information \(I(X; Y)\) as possible*. The residual loss after training, \(H(Y \mid X)\), is the irreducible noise in the data that no model of any capacity can remove, and it serves as a natural lower bound on the training loss.

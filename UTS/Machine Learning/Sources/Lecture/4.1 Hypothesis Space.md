# Introduction: What is a Hypothesis Space?

A typical machine learning task is the search for a function that maps inputs to desired outputs. However, we cannot search through *all* possible functions. That would be both computationally infeasible and statistically useless. More specifically, "be a mapping from input domain \(\mathcal{X}\) to output domain \(\mathcal{Y}\)" is an extremely broad requirement, that unnecessarily includes a vast population of useless candidate functions --- shortly we will be on the topic of what counts as "useless" and "vast". For now, the intuition of the core idea is that limiting our search space is essential for learning to be possible, and its contribution is twofold: It makes the search possible, by reducing it to focus in a range of a manageable size; and it makes the selected function more likely to be meaningful and relevant to the task, by excluding the irregular and random candidates entering the search space in the first place.

The figure illustrates this point. The task in this example is the well-known Iris classification problem. We simplify the example for the sake of illustration, and only consider two features (sepal length and sepal width, both being normalized to \([0, 1]\)) and two classes (Setosa and Versicolor). The training samples are shown as colored circles, where the color indicates the class label. The background color (looks like random pixel colors) represents that at each location in the input space, a model has estimated the likelihood of the corresponding input belonging to the Versicolor class. The model is from a very broad hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{RND}}\) --- it allows the members to output any likelihood between \(0\%\) and \(100\%\) for any input --- hence the seemingly random background colors. The model shown in the figure is "trained" against the training samples --- by simply forcing the model to output \(100\%\) or \(0\%\) at the locations of the training samples, depending on their class labels. As \(\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{RND}}\) has no constraints, it is fair to say that the above "training" is legitimate and outputs a member function that does belong to \(\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{RND}}\) (and fits perfectly to the training data as well.) However, one can easily see that the model is completely useless for any new input, as it assigns random likelihoods to inputs that are even very close to known samples.

This is an intuitive demonstration of how not limiting the behaviour patterns of the function family can lead to a disastrous outcome. A short takeaway message from this opening discussion should be:

*Designing the model family \(\mathcal{H}\) is a LIMITING action; and the LIMITATION is not a "DESPITE OF" but a "THANK TO" factor for the success of learning.*
## Hypothesis Space Design: Three Fundamental Requirements

Recap the learning problem:

\[ h^* = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} E_{\text{in}}(h; D) \]

Learning is to select the best function \(h^*\) from a set \(\mathcal{H}\), typically by minimizing some empirical error (loss) on the training data \(D: \{\mathbf{x}_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N\). This pre-defined family of functions, denoted as \(\mathcal{H}\), is called the **Hypothesis Space**.

It is intuitive and easy to see that, as the range of candidate functions, \(\mathcal{H}\) must maintain some level of capacity to describe the underlying relationship between inputs and outputs, so it can be reasonably applied to practical tasks. The more subtle issue is often related to the other side: overly capable hypothesis spaces are also problematic. As demonstrated in the discussion above, if \(\mathcal{H}\) is too broad and does not impose any constraints on its member candidates, the minimization process is unlikely to yield meaningful working functions. Here we may also take a more intuitive perspective of "machine design": if after observing the training dataset, a machine only pins down the outputs at exactly the locations given by the samples in the input space, and can freely produce any output at locations that are not specified before, then the "observations" \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}\) may simply be renamed "addresses", and the machine has merely recorded the information associated with those addresses for later retrieval. This is precisely how a lookup table works: the machine is essentially a hard disk or memory, storing information at given addresses while leaving all other locations undefined. This represents the extreme case when the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) contains an overly flexible family of functions, and the learning process is reduced to a simple memorization of the training data.

Therefore, designing a good hypothesis space is an art of balancing trade-offs. There are three fundamental requirements that need to be considered when designing or choosing \(\mathcal{H}\) for a task:

- 

**Expressivity (Is it capable enough?)**: The space \(\mathcal{H}\) must be large and rich enough so that there exist members in \(\mathcal{H}\) that can approximate the target underlying relationship \(f_{\text{true}}\) sufficiently well. By "well approximated" we mean that there exists at least one function \(h \in \mathcal{H}\), such that \(E_{\text{out}}(h)\) is within a predetermined acceptable range. Note that usually, one does NOT require or even expect that the target function \(f_{\text{true}}\) itself is included in \(\mathcal{H}\).

The difference between the "best" approximator and the true function is called the approximation error, or in some contexts, the bias. Formally,

\[ \varepsilon_{\text{approx}}(\mathcal{H}) = \inf_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \left\{ E_{\text{out}}(h) \right\} = E_{\text{out}}(h^*) \]

where the second equality is due to the definition of \(h^*\) above. Note that \(\varepsilon_{\text{approx}}(\mathcal{H})\) is no longer a property of any single function \(h\), but a property of the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) as a whole. It characterises the best possible performance achievable *within* \(\mathcal{H}\), regardless of how the learning algorithm performs. In this sense, it reflects an intrinsic limitation imposed by the design choice of \(\mathcal{H}\) itself, rather than by the learning process.

- 

**Generalization (Is its capacity excessive?)**: The space \(\mathcal{H}\) must not be excessively complex. An overly flexible hypothesis space risks reducing the learning system to, in effect, a sophisticated storage mechanism that retrieves memorised training examples rather than generalising to unseen inputs. Here, it is one matter that \(\mathcal{H}\) contains well-performing candidates; it is a separate matter whether the available training data \(D\) provides sufficient evidence to identify such candidates and distinguish them from poor ones.

This sensitivity is formally captured by the notion of **variance**. As the term suggests, it concerns the degree of uncertainty --- specifically, the extent to which the outcome of the learning process fluctuates depending on a stochastic element.

Pause and reflect: what is the stochastic element here?

In the context of learning, this uncertainty arises because the training dataset \(D\) is a random realisation drawn from the underlying data distribution \(P_D\). At the stage of experimental design, one may fix the configuration --- for instance, the sample size \(N\) of \(D\). However, when the learning procedure is actually carried out, one is committed to a single random realisation of the dataset. In the language of statistical ensembles: although an \(N\)-sample ensemble admits infinitely many possible realisations, only one such realisation is observed in any given learning session.

To make this precise, consider a thought experiment: imagine an ensemble of learning sessions, each sharing identical configuration but conducted with an independently drawn dataset \(D^{(k)}\) (say, right after the learning plan is fixed, and the first sub-task of data collection is going to begin, we branched out into many parallel universes, if you like). Each session will in general produce a different selected hypothesis. The variance quantifies the expected variability of outcomes across this ensemble --- it is in this sense that the term is used here.

\[ \varepsilon_{\text{variance}}(\mathcal{H}) = \mathbb{E}_{D \sim P_D} \left[ \left( E_{\text{out}}(h^*(D)) - \varepsilon_{\text{approx}}(\mathcal{H}) \right)^2 \right] \]

Here, \(h^*(D) = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} E_{\text{in}}(h; D)\) denotes the hypothesis selected by the learning algorithm when trained on a specific dataset \(D\). The variance \(\varepsilon_{\text{variance}}(\mathcal{H})\) thus quantifies the expected squared deviation between the best achievable performance within \(\mathcal{H}\) --- as characterised by the approximation error \(\varepsilon_{\text{approx}}(\mathcal{H})\) --- and the actual out-of-sample performance of the hypothesis returned by the algorithm on a randomly drawn dataset. It therefore reflects how sensitively the learning outcome depends on the particular realisation of the training data.

- 

**Learnability (Is its capacity practically realisable?)**: There is one gap in the above discussion that we have not yet addressed: when discussing the variance, we mentioned that for a given dataset \(D\), there is a corresponding function \(h^*(D)\), which is the minimizer of the empirical error \(E_{\text{in}}(h; D)\) within \(\mathcal{H}\). We took this \(h^*(D)\) for granted, but in practice, it must actually be found from \(\mathcal{H}\) by some concrete computational procedure. This is determined by many factors, including:

How one actually represents a member in \(\mathcal{H}\) in a computer implementation of the learning system.
- What is the landscape of the empirical error \(E_{\text{in}}(h; D)\) over \(\mathcal{H}\)? Is it convex or non-convex? Are there many local minima?
- Does the problem admit an analytical solution, i.e. can one derive a closed-form formula for \(h^*(D)\)? Or does it require an iterative numerical optimisation procedure? (In most practical cases, it is the latter.)
- In the optimisation, at a particular step, is there an effective way to compute the change of the current \(h\) within \(\mathcal{H}\) that can effectively reduce the empirical error? (This is the key question for the success of Gradient Descent, which is a pillar of modern machine learning.) If the direction of effective change can be computed, then the next question is: how much to change in a particular step?
- Is there an effective way to determine when to stop the iteration, i.e. when the current \(h\) is good enough? Is the stopping condition directly related to the empirical error \(E_{\text{in}}(h; D)\), or is it related to some other cues that can be recorded or derived during the optimisation process?
- Is there an effective way to avoid getting stuck in local minima? This question is of course related both to how to determine if the current \(h\) is good enough, and to how to compute the next step.

These three requirements are often in tension. Expanding \(\mathcal{H}\) improves Expressivity but typically hurts Generalization and makes Learnability much harder. The evolution of machine learning models is largely the history of finding clever ways to design \(\mathcal{H}\) that strike a practical balance between these three pillars. ## The Simplest Example: The Linear Perceptron

To make this concrete, let us look at the simplest possible hypothesis space: the linear perceptron. For an input vector \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)\), the model predicts the output by taking a linear combination of the inputs and passing it through an activation function \(\phi\):

\[ \text{Pred}(\mathbf{x}) = \phi\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i + b\right) \]

Here, the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) is the set of all possible functions parameterized by the weights \(w_i\) and the bias \(b\).

How does this \(\mathcal{H}\) fare against our three requirements?

- **Expressivity**: Fair. The linear perceptron can perform well on tasks where the decision boundary is a hyperplane in the space of observations, such as the classic Iris classification problem. However, it fails on tasks that require non-linear decision boundaries. For example, the XOR problem is a classic case where the linear perceptron cannot find a solution, because it can only draw straight lines (or hyperplanes) to separate data.
- **Learnability**: Excellent. The optimization landscape for linear models (with appropriate loss functions) is convex, meaning we can reliably and efficiently find the global optimum.
- **Generalization**: Very good. Because the model only has \(n+1\) parameters, its capacity to memorize noise is highly restricted. It rarely overfits unless the dataset is extremely small.

So if one starts from the linear family of learning systems, an obvious direction to improve is to expand the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) for richer expressivity. Of course, the real question is: how to do this without sacrificing learnability and generalization too much?

### Historical Remark

The failure of the linear perceptron on the XOR problem is a watershed moment in the history of machine learning. In the 1950s, the demonstration that a linear perceptron could autonomously learn to classify linearly separable patterns generated considerable optimism about the prospects of intelligent machines. However, it was soon recognised --- most influentially through the analysis of Minsky and Papert (1969) --- that the linear perceptron is fundamentally incapable of representing even the simplest non-linear relationships, of which XOR is the canonical example. This realisation contributed to a prolonged period of scepticism regarding the broader potential of machine learning, commonly referred to as the "AI winter."

The tide turned in the 1980s, when it was demonstrated that, with a sufficiently clever architectural design, the hypothesis family could indeed be expanded to represent non-linear patterns --- and, remarkably, that it could in principle approximate *any* continuous function to arbitrary precision (the Universal Approximation Theorem). This result reinvigorated the field and sparked sustained interest in three interconnected directions: establishing rigorous theoretical foundations for expressivity (clarifying, in particular, why a powerful hypothesis space need not degenerate into the kind of arbitrary family discussed earlier); developing effective learning algorithms capable of searching such spaces; and designing more expressive yet tractable architectures.

The history of the field has since seen several further cycles of enthusiasm and disillusionment. At the time of writing, we find ourselves in the midst of one of the most rapid and consequential periods of progress the field has ever witnessed.

## Excellent on Paper, Untrustworthy in Practice

We begin our study of Machine Learning by addressing the elephant in the room: if Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) systems are so powerful, why are they often viewed with deep suspicion?

Consider the state of autonomous driving. By many statistical benchmarks, autonomous systems (such as Waymo) boast accident rates in "rider-only miles" that are significantly lower than human benchmarks. Similarly, in medical imaging, AI models frequently surpass the average performance of clinicians in metrics like sensitivity and specificity for detecting conditions like lung nodules or breast cancer.

Yet, surveys consistently show a lack of trust. Only a small fraction of households express willingness to ride in fully autonomous vehicles, and clinicians remain skeptical of diagnoses made entirely by AI.

## The Nature of the Distrust

The root of this distrust is not usually the quantity of errors (reliability) or the severity of the consequences. Rather, it is the **nature** of the failures.

When a human fails, we can usually understand *why*. A driver might be distracted or tired; a doctor might miss a subtle cue. These failures fit into a causal model of the world that we understand.

In contrast, AI systems often exhibit:

- **Unpredictable Failure Conditions:** Models may perform flawlessly in difficult scenarios but fail in trivial ones due to noise or perturbations that a human would ignore.
- **Unexplainable Failure Reasons:** Because these systems are "trained" from existing data rather than built from first principles (physics or logic), we often cannot trace a specific error back to a specific faulty component.

This leads to a perception that ML systems are "stupid" in a way that is alien to human intelligence. We do not know *when* they will mistake a stop sign for a speed limit sign, nor do we know exactly *why*.

## Why Bother?

If these systems are fundamentally opaque and occasionally erratic, why do we use them in the first place? The answer lies in our ambition to tackle "real-world" problems, where the complexity of the environment defies traditional programming or any formal language.

For many tasks—visual recognition, natural language understanding, complex strategy games—we simply do not know how to write down the explicit logical rules required to solve the problem. We cannot explicitly program a "cat detector" by writing if-else statements about pixel values.

We need a different approach. Instead of programming the *solution*, we program a machine to *learn* the solution from data.

We have seen that the gradient descent update of model parameters,

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \mathbf{g}_t = \nabla_\theta J(\theta_t), \label{eq:vanilla_gd} \]

is derived from a reasonable heuristic: it follows the *direction* of steepest descent, which is computed at the current parameter values \(\theta_t\) via the first-order Taylor expansion of \(J\). What it does not account for is the *neighbourhood geometry* of the loss surface. The omission is mostly about the range in which the first-order approximation is an effective delegate of the true dependence of \(J\) on \(\theta\). The vanilla gradient descent (GD) is suboptimal for some practical tasks due to these limitations.

One common behaviour of vanilla GD that needs improvement is oscillation in narrow valleys. Consider a loss surface where curvature in one parameter direction is much larger than in another. This configuration is common near saddle points and throughout deep networks. Intuitively, the curvature in a direction determines how far the first-order approximation is valid in that direction. Because gradient descent scales the step identically in every direction, the component of \(\mathbf{g}_t\) pointing *across* the steep wall of the valley is large and drives the iterate back and forth in a zig-zag, while the component pointing *along* the valley toward the minimum is small and makes only slow progress. Increasing \(\eta\) amplifies the across-valley oscillation without proportionally accelerating the along-valley descent.

Another effective improvement is to adapt the step size per dimension. Simply put, it is to let slowly-updated parameters take larger steps and quickly-updated parameters take smaller steps. Superficially, this seems to be baseless guesswork, but this argument is actually grounded in the geometry of the loss surface. Consider networks with sparse inputs—language models, for instance, where a word embedding parameter participates in the computation graph only for training examples that contain a rare word. Then both the gradient and the curvature (Hessian) in that dimension are small due to the scarcity of related training examples. That is, small gradient also means flat terrain along the direction of travel. The flatness is *not* captured by the first-order approximation. Vanilla GD, however, takes a step with a global proportionality constant \(\eta\) that is the same for every dimension. A global \(\eta\) chosen to be safe for the frequent, large-gradient dimensions will be systematically too small for the sparse dimensions—not because those parameters matter less, but because the loss surface is correspondingly flatter in those directions and a larger step is geometrically safe. Per-dimension step scaling corrects this imbalance: dividing by a quantity proportional to the historical gradient magnitude approximates the inverse curvature factor \(1/\bar{H}\), recovering a Newton-like update without explicitly computing any second derivatives.

The next subsections present optimiser designs that improve gradient descent practical performance. Momentum addresses oscillation by giving the iterate a memory of recent gradient directions. AdaGrad addresses non-uniform magnitudes by adapting the step size per dimension, but it introduces a new problem of over-estimation of the second-order moment of the gradient. RMSProp repairs that problem by replacing cumulation with a sliding average. Adam absorbs both mechanisms and adds a correction that improves the early steps, when no sufficient history has yet been encountered and the higher-order moment estimates are inaccurate.

## Momentum: Suppressing Oscillation by Accumulating Velocity

The intuition comes from classical mechanics. A ball rolling down a hill accumulates velocity over time; small bumps perpendicular to its trajectory are averaged out while the sustained downhill force continues to accelerate it forward. The optimizer mimics this by maintaining a velocity vector \(\mathbf{m}_t \in \mathbb{R}^p\) that mixes the current gradient with all preceding ones:

\[\begin{align} \mathbf{m}_{t+1} &= \beta\, \mathbf{m}_t + \mathbf{g}_t, \label{eq:mom_v} \\ \theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \eta\, \mathbf{m}_{t+1}, \label{eq:mom_update} \end{align}\]

where \(\beta \in [0,1)\) is the momentum coefficient, typically \(0.9\), and \(\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}\). Unrolling the recursion \eqref{eq:mom_v} shows that the velocity at step \(t+1\) is an exponentially weighted sum of all past gradients:

\[\begin{align} \mathbf{m}_{t+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{t} \beta^{t-k}\, \mathbf{g}_k. \label{eq:mom_expand} \end{align}\]

The effect on oscillation is direct. In the oscillating direction, consecutive gradients point in opposite signs: the contribution of \(\mathbf{g}_k\) is partially cancelled by \(\mathbf{g}_{k+1}\). Their weighted sum in \(\mathbf{m}\) is therefore smaller than either individual gradient, damping the across-valley component. In the descent direction, gradients consistently share the same sign: successive contributions reinforce each other in \(\mathbf{m}\), producing a larger effective step than any single gradient alone. The net result is a trajectory that descends more directly toward the minimum with less wasted lateral motion.

The price of this memory is a new hyperparameter \(\beta\) and a risk of overshooting a sharp minimum if \(\beta\) or \(\eta\) are set too large. Momentum does not, however, address the second failure mode: the update \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \mathbf{m}_{t+1}\) still applies the same global step scale \(\eta\) to every parameter dimension. Per-dimension adaptation requires a separate mechanism.

## AdaGrad: Adaptive Per-Dimension Step Scaling

Momentum addresses the geometry of the trajectory but not the disparity in gradient magnitudes across dimensions. For the sparse-gradient problem, the key observation is that parameters which consistently receive large gradients should take smaller effective steps to avoid overshoot, while those receiving small or infrequent gradients should take larger effective steps to make progress despite the scarcity of signal. This calls for a step size that is inversely proportional to the historical magnitude of the gradient for each dimension independently.

AdaGrad (Duchi et al., 2011) achieves this by accumulating the squared gradient component-wise since the start of training. Writing \(g_{t,j}\) for the \(j\)-th component of \(\mathbf{g}_t\), the update is

\[\begin{align} G_{t+1,j} &= G_{t,j} + g_{t,j}^2, \label{eq:adagrad_accum} \\ \theta_{t+1,j} &= \theta_{t,j} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{G_{t+1,j}} + \epsilon}\; g_{t,j}, \label{eq:adagrad_update} \end{align}\]

with \(G_{0,j} = 0\) and \(\epsilon > 0\) (typically \(10^{-8}\)) to prevent division by zero. In vector notation, letting \(\mathbf{G}_t \in \mathbb{R}^p\) collect all accumulators and writing \(\oslash\) for componentwise division,

\[\begin{align} \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\; \mathbf{g}_t \oslash \bigl(\sqrt{\mathbf{G}_{t+1}} + \epsilon\bigr). \label{eq:adagrad_vec} \end{align}\]

The advantages flow directly from the denominator structure. A dimension \(j\) that rarely receives a gradient has \(G_{t,j}\) growing slowly, so \(\eta/\sqrt{G_{t,j}}\) remains large: when the sparse dimension does receive a gradient it takes an appropriately large step. A dimension that receives large gradients at every step has \(G_{t,j}\) growing rapidly and its effective step shrinks to prevent overshooting. No additional hyperparameter beyond the global scale \(\eta\) is needed, and the adaptation is automatic.

The weakness is equally visible in \eqref{eq:adagrad_accum}. Because each \(G_{t,j}\) is a running sum of non-negative terms it can only increase, so the effective step \(\eta/\sqrt{G_{t,j}}\) decreases monotonically and converges to zero as \(t \to \infty\). For convex problems with a finite optimal solution this is benign: the step naturally shrinks as the iterate converges. For the non-convex loss surfaces of deep learning, where training must continue for tens of thousands of steps and the iterate must traverse many different regions of \(\Theta\), the monotone decay means the optimizer is effectively frozen long before the parameters have converged.

AdaGrad applied to a two-dimensional problem with very different gradient frequencies per dimension. Left: the accumulated denominator \(G_t\) grows without bound in the frequently-updated dimension while remaining small in the sparse one. Right: the resulting effective step sizes diverge, with the frequently-updated dimension's step approaching zero after a few hundred iterations.

## RMSProp: Replacing Cumulation with a Sliding Average

The root cause of AdaGrad's decay is that \(G_{t,j}\) uses a *cumulative* sum: every squared gradient ever seen, no matter how long ago or how far from the current iterate, contributes permanently to the denominator. The fix is to give the denominator a finite memory by replacing the sum with an exponential moving average.

RMSProp (Hinton, 2012) modifies only the denominator computation:

\[\begin{align} \mathbf{v}_{t+1} &= \rho\, \mathbf{v}_t + (1-\rho)\, \mathbf{g}_t \odot \mathbf{g}_t, \label{eq:rmsprop_v} \\ \theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \eta\; \mathbf{g}_t \oslash \bigl(\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_{t+1}} + \epsilon\bigr), \label{eq:rmsprop_update} \end{align}\]

where \(\odot\) denotes componentwise multiplication, \(\rho \in [0,1)\) (typically \(0.9\)) controls how quickly the past is forgotten, and \(\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}\). Unrolling the recursion gives

\[\begin{align} \mathbf{v}_{t+1} = (1-\rho)\sum_{k=0}^{t} \rho^{t-k}\; \mathbf{g}_k \odot \mathbf{g}_k, \label{eq:rmsprop_expand} \end{align}\]

so the denominator reflects a weighted average of recent squared gradients, with weights \((1-\rho)\rho^{t-k}\) decaying exponentially in lag \(k\). Gradients from many steps ago are down-weighted toward zero, so the effective step \(\eta/\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_t}\) remains responsive throughout a long run.

The relationship to AdaGrad is transparent. Setting \(\rho = 0\) recovers a single-step memory (no past history), while \(\rho \to 1\) recovers the cumulative sum of AdaGrad; any \(\rho \in (0,1)\) introduces forgetting and resolves the dying-step problem. The choice of \(\rho\) controls the effective window of gradient history: smaller \(\rho\) forgets faster and reacts more sharply to recent curvature changes, while larger \(\rho\) smooths over more steps.

RMSProp adapts the step size per dimension and avoids monotone decay, but it operates on gradient *magnitudes* independently for each dimension and does nothing to reduce oscillation along directions of high curvature. The two problems—oscillation and per-dimension scaling—are addressed by separate mechanisms. The natural next step is to combine them.

Effective per-dimension step size over training iterations for AdaGrad and RMSProp on the same gradient sequence. AdaGrad's step decreases monotonically and approaches zero; RMSProp's step fluctuates around a stable value because the exponential moving average forgets early gradient history.

## Adam: Combining Momentum and RMSProp with Bias Correction

Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation, Kingma and Ba, 2015) brings together the momentum estimate for direction smoothing and the second-moment estimate for per-dimension step scaling, and adds one further refinement: a correction for the bias that initializing both estimates at zero introduces in the early steps.

Both estimates are updated as exponential moving averages, mirroring the structures of \eqref{eq:mom_v} and \eqref{eq:rmsprop_v}:

\[\begin{align} \mathbf{m}_{t+1} &= \beta_1\, \mathbf{m}_t + (1-\beta_1)\, \mathbf{g}_t, \label{eq:adam_m} \\ \mathbf{v}_{t+1} &= \beta_2\, \mathbf{v}_t + (1-\beta_2)\, \mathbf{g}_t \odot \mathbf{g}_t, \label{eq:adam_v} \end{align}\]

with \(\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}\) and default coefficients \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\). The first moment \(\mathbf{m}_t\) smooths the gradient direction as momentum does; the second moment \(\mathbf{v}_t\) estimates the per-dimension gradient variance as RMSProp does.

Initializing at zero introduces a systematic underestimate in the early steps. At \(t = 1\), \(\mathbf{m}_1 = (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_0\), which is only a fraction \((1-\beta_1)\) of the first gradient rather than an estimate of the expected gradient. More generally, for any stationary gradient distribution the expected value of the running average satisfies

\[\begin{align} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = \bigl(1 - \beta_1^t\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}], \label{eq:adam_bias} \end{align}\]

so \(\mathbf{m}_t\) underestimates the true mean by the factor \((1-\beta_1^t)\). The same bias affects \(\mathbf{v}_t\) with \(\beta_2^t\). Adam removes both biases before the parameter update:

\[\begin{align} \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t+1} &= \frac{\mathbf{m}_{t+1}}{1 - \beta_1^{t+1}}, \label{eq:adam_mhat} \\ \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1} &= \frac{\mathbf{v}_{t+1}}{1 - \beta_2^{t+1}}, \label{eq:adam_vhat} \end{align}\]

and the parameter update uses the corrected estimates:

\[\begin{align} \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t+1}} + \epsilon}\; \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t+1}. \label{eq:adam_update} \end{align}\]

The correction factors \(1/(1 - \beta_1^t)\) and \(1/(1 - \beta_2^t)\) both approach \(1\) as \(t\) grows. For \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), the first-moment correction is approximately \(10\) at \(t=1\) and falls below \(1.05\) by \(t \approx 30\). After this warm-up period the correction is negligible and Adam behaves as the direct combination of momentum and RMSProp. The correction matters most at the very start: without it, both \(\hat{\mathbf{m}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) would be severely deflated, causing the optimizer to take excessively small steps in the first few dozen iterations precisely when the gradient signal is typically the clearest.

Optimizer trajectories on the Rosenbrock surface \(J(x,y) = (1-x)^2 + 10(y - x^2)^2\), starting from the same point (square), with the global minimum at \((1,1)\) (star). Vanilla GD progresses very slowly; momentum makes larger steps toward the minimum but curves sharply in the narrow banana-shaped valley; RMSProp scales each dimension separately and navigates the valley more efficiently; Adam combines both adaptations and converges most directly under the same number of steps.

## A Comparative View

Each optimizer in this sequence is defined by the specific defect it repairs in the one before it, and reading the update equations side by side makes those relationships explicit.

Vanilla GD \eqref{eq:vanilla_gd} applies a uniform step \(\eta\) to every parameter dimension and discards all previous gradient information at each step. Momentum \eqref{eq:mom_update} retains past gradient directions through the velocity \(\mathbf{m}_t\), suppressing oscillations, but still applies the same global scale \(\eta\) to all dimensions. AdaGrad \eqref{eq:adagrad_vec} introduces per-dimension step scaling through the accumulated denominator \(\sqrt{\mathbf{G}_t}\), but because \(\mathbf{G}_t\) grows without bound the effective step shrinks to zero. RMSProp \eqref{eq:rmsprop_update} replaces the cumulative sum in \(\mathbf{G}_t\) with the exponential moving average \(\mathbf{v}_t\), stabilizing the effective step size without sacrificing per-dimension adaptation. Adam \eqref{eq:adam_update} combines the momentum of \(\mathbf{m}_t\) with the second-moment scaling of \(\mathbf{v}_t\), and adds bias correction to ensure that the early steps, when both moving averages are deflated from their zero initialization, are not artificially small.

In practice, Adam with \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\), \(\epsilon = 10^{-8}\), and a learning rate \(\eta \in [10^{-4}, 10^{-3}]\) serves as the default for a wide range of deep learning architectures. The succession of variants traced in this section is not merely of historical interest: each name encodes a specific update-equation structure, and diagnosing an optimizer's behavior in a new problem often amounts to identifying which of the four failure modes—oscillation, non-uniform magnitude, decay, and initialization bias—is dominant.

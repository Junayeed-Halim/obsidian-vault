# Introduction: Learning as Optimization

In the previous modules we have set up the core components of a machine learning pipeline:

- The hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\), which constrains the family of candidate functions;
- The criterion (loss function and risk), which measures how well a candidate \(h \in \mathcal{H}\) fits the data.

Recall that the learning problem is *defined* as Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)

\[ h^* = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \;\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\bigl(h(x_i),\; y_i\bigr) \]

where \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\) is the observed examples. The remaining question is an operational one: how to solve the ERM problem, a.k.a. how do we actually find the best function \(h^*\)?

This is the role of the learning algorithm—the computational procedure that searches through \(\mathcal{H}\) and delivers a concrete working model.

Of course, “solving”, or the \(\arg\min\) in the ERM equation, can mean very different things depending on the specific design of \(\mathcal{H}\) and the loss functional. Roughly speaking, there are two broad routes to finding \(h^*\):

- **Parametric**: There exists a concrete mapping between a parameter space \(\Theta\) and the function space \(\mathcal{H}\), such that each \(\theta \in \Theta\) defines a function \(h_\theta \in \mathcal{H}\). Searching for \(h^*\) is then equivalent to searching for the best parameters \(\theta^*\). This family includes very popular models such as linear regression, logistic regression and deep neural networks.
- **Data-centric (non-parametric)**: A model can be defined by a description of how to construct the output mapping \(x \mapsto \hat{y}\) for arbitrary input \(x\). There is no associated parameter space \(\Theta\) in this procedure. In the context of learning, the construction is with respect to the training data, which propagates the observed information to the prediction function. This family also includes important models such as kernel methods.

Roughly speaking, the parametric and data-centric routes can be understood with respect to the two types of hypothesis spaces we have discussed: the former is more natural for explicit combination of basis functions, where the function is defined by a fixed set of parameters; the latter is more natural for implicit combination, where the function is defined by a procedure that directly consults the training data.

Let us take a moment to discuss the two routes at a high level before studying a few concrete examples. A large class of practical models (including modern deep networks) fall into the parametric category. The learning algorithm is essentially an optimization procedure in the parameter space \(\Theta\). This is a rich topic with a large body of methods and insights. Depending on the structure of \(\mathcal{H}\) and \(L\), the tools range from closed-form solutions (e.g. the normal equation for linear regression) to first-order conditions such as the KKT conditions for constrained problems, to iterative gradient-based methods for non-convex landscapes. The dominant thread of this module concerns the latter: how to navigate the loss landscape \(J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i L(h_\theta(x_i), y_i)\) when a closed-form minimizer is unavailable.

A key feature of this route is that after training, the working function \(h_{\theta^*}\) is not explicitly tied to the training data points. The model has “internalized” the data into its parameters, and the training dataset can be discarded. The learning model will be deployed as a standalone function at prediction time.

The data-centric route may at first glance seem to involve less “learning”, since there is no explicit iterative procedure of searching through a space. Some models may even have essentially zero empirical risk on the training set, and therefore no explicit training is involved at all. The correct interpretation, however, is that the searching *has been done*—just in the function space \(\mathcal{H}\) directly, rather than indirectly through a parameter space. After all, the ultimate task is to construct a mechanism \(\mathcal{X} \mapsto \mathcal{Y}\), and these models do that by referring to the training data for each prediction. The result is often elegant and theoretically well-grounded. The downside is practical: prediction time typically scales with \(N\) (each new query must consult the stored training set), and these models face challenges scaling to large datasets.

We now examine concrete examples of each route, beginning with the parametric case before turning to the data-centric family.

## Learning a Parametric Model: Linear Regression

The simplest parametric model is ordinary linear regression. It is a special case where the optimization need not be iterative—it can be solved in closed form.

Model and training.

Assume the hypothesis space consists of linear functions \(h_w(x) = x^\top w\), parameterized by \(w \in \mathbb{R}^d\). The ERM problem with squared loss becomes

\[ \min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^d} \;\|Xw - y\|^2 \]

where \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}\) stacks the training inputs row-wise and \(y \in \mathbb{R}^N\) is the target vector. Expanding the objective gives

\[ \|Xw - y\|^2 = (Xw - y)^\top (Xw - y) = w^\top X^\top X w - 2 w^\top X^\top y + y^\top y \]

The key idea is to consider a *necessary condition* for optimality. Of the following two statements:

- **A**: Some \(w^*\) minimizes the objective, and
- **B**: If any component \(w^*_i\) is perturbed by any small amount \(\epsilon\), the objective does NOT decrease (if it does, the current \(w^*\) is not optimal).

Since **A \(\Rightarrow\) B**, one can use **B** as a necessary condition for optimality **A**. This is a common strategy: **B** is NOT equivalent to **A**, but it is *constructive* and computable, while **A** is declarative and not directly computable.

The argument proceeds as follows. Consider the dependency of the objective on a particular component \(w_i\), temporarily denoted \(L^*(w_i)\), where the superscript \(^*\) indicates all other components are fixed. Then not only is \(L^*(w_i)\) non-decreasing when \(w_i\) is perturbed, it also cannot be *increasing*: if \(L^*(w_i)\) increases when \(w_i\) is perturbed by an infinitesimal positive quantity, we can perturb it negatively to decrease the objective, contradicting optimality. Formally, the local Taylor expansion gives

\[ L^*(w_i + \epsilon) = L^*(w_i) + \epsilon \frac{d L^*(w_i)}{d w_i} + O(\epsilon^2) \]

As \(\epsilon \to 0\), the high-order term \(O(\epsilon^2)\) is negligible, and the sign of the perturbation is determined by \(\frac{d L^*(w_i)}{d w_i}\). If this derivative is nonzero, one can always choose \(\epsilon\) to decrease the objective, contradicting optimality. Therefore the only possibility is

\[ \frac{d L^*(w_i)}{d w_i} = 0 \]

This first-order necessary condition applies to all components of \(w^*\), giving

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial w_i}\bigl(w^\top X^\top X w - 2 w^\top X^\top y \bigr) = 0, \quad \text{for all } i \]

(The constant \(y^\top y\) is dropped as it does not depend on \(w\).)

Rather than differentiating the quadratic form component by component, we take a more elegant route: decompose the gradient via the model's prediction \(\hat{y} = Xw \in \mathbb{R}^N\). First, the gradient with respect to \(\hat{y}\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{y}} \|\hat{y} - y\|^2 = 2(\hat{y} - y) = 2(Xw - y) \]

Then, the sensitivity of \(\hat{y}\) to \(w\) is captured by the Jacobian matrix, governing the sensitivity of *multiple* outputs with respect to *multiple* control variables:

\[ \left[\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial w}\right]_{i,j} = \frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial w_j} = X_{i,j} \]

In other words, the Jacobian is simply \(X\). Applying the chain rule:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial w} \|\hat{y} - y\|^2 = \left[\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial w}\right]^\top \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{y}} \|\hat{y} - y\|^2 = X^\top \cdot 2(Xw - y) = 2(X^\top X w - X^\top y) \]

Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equation:

\[ X^\top X\, w^* = X^\top y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y \]

This is a closed-form solution obtained by solving a linear system.

This is a long route to derive the formula for linear regression. But stepping back, there are two take-away points from this calculation:

- Shift the focus from optimality (declarative and global) to first-order dynamics (constructive and local). The price to pay is that the necessary condition is not sufficient, so we must be careful when applying it.
- The gradient can be computed by applying the chain rule, which involves the Jacobian matrix of the model prediction with respect to the parameters.

These two principles are not incidental to this example—they are the *conceptual backbone* of the entire optimization module. The first principle, shifting from a global declaration of optimality to a local, computable first-order condition, is precisely what makes gradient-based training possible for arbitrary models. The second principle, computing gradients end-to-end via the chain rule and Jacobian matrices, is exactly what backpropagation formalizes for deep networks. We will return to both repeatedly.

After training: no memory of individual data points.

Once \(w^* \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is computed, the training set \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}\) can be discarded entirely. Prediction for a new point \(x_*\) is simply \(\hat{y} = x_*^\top w^*\)—a fixed inner product of cost \(O(d)\), entirely independent of \(N\). The model has “distilled” the information from \(N\) examples into a \(d\)-dimensional parameter vector. This is the essential contrast to data-centric methods.

## Data-Centric Models

Simple data-centric models are “automatically trained”: once the training data is provided, the working prediction function \(x \mapsto \hat{y}\) is structurally anchored to it. The answer can come from consulting the nearest neighbours or weighting all training targets by similarity. The price for this convenience is that the training data *remains part of the model after learning*. No fixed-size parameter vector distills the training set; the data itself is the representation and must be referenced at prediction time. Intuitively, the data-centric route bypasses the “compression of data into a parameter” step, and for each incoming query, a cost is paid to compare it against the training data to make a prediction.

There are middle grounds between the two routes, where the model is still data-centric but requires an explicit optimization step to determine *which part of the data* to retain as the carriers of the prediction function—such as the support vectors in an SVM.

Below we illustrate two simple data-centric models, nearest neighbour and naïve kernel regression. We also provide a brief introduction to SVM to show how it sits in the two-route picture.

### \(k\)-Nearest Neighbours (KNN)

KNN is perhaps the simplest data-centric model. It stores the entire training set and makes predictions by local voting.

Mechanism.

Given a new input \(x_*\), KNN

- Finds the \(k\) training points \(\{x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(k)}\}\) closest to \(x_*\) (under some distance metric, typically Euclidean);
- For classification: returns the majority label among the \(k\) neighbours; for regression: returns the average of their target values.

Why data-centric?

The “model” is just the training set itself. There is no parameter to tune (aside from the choice of \(k\), which is a hyperparameter selected before training, not learned from data). The prediction rule is entirely determined by the data layout.

KNN classification. Left: \(k=1\) (decision boundary tightly follows individual points). Right: \(k=3\) (smoother boundary from majority voting).

Expressivity and limitations.

- **Universal consistency**: As \(N \to \infty\) and \(k/N \to 0\) (while \(k \to \infty\)), KNN converges to the Bayes-optimal classifier under mild conditions. In this asymptotic sense, KNN is “expressive enough.”
- **Curse of dimensionality**: Determining the right \(K\) is non-trivial. One alternative is to set \(K\) adaptively—instead of a fixed \(K\), include all training points within radius \(r\) of \(x_*\). This sidesteps the discrete choice of \(K\), but does not resolve the fundamental difficulty. In high dimensions, the volume of a ball of unit radius vanishes, so to have a meaningful number of neighbours, \(r\) must grow with \(d\). But a larger \(r\) makes the neighbourhood less local and the notion of distance less discriminative between “near” and “far” points. A radius small enough to be selective captures almost no points; a radius large enough to capture any neighbours captures nearly all of them. This is a manifestation of the curse of dimensionality.
- **No compression**: The entire training set must be stored and queried at prediction time. The computational cost of prediction scales with \(N\).

### Kernel Methods

The key idea: similarity as a prediction mechanism.

Let \(k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) be a kernel function that quantifies the similarity between two inputs. Popular choices include the Gaussian (RBF) kernel \(k(x, x') = \exp\bigl(-\|x - x'\|^2 / 2\sigma^2\bigr)\), polynomial kernels \((x^{\top} x' + c)^p\), and the linear kernel \(x^{\top} x'\).

The intuition is natural: if \(x_*\) is similar to some training point \(x_i\), then \(y_i\) should carry more weight in the prediction for \(x_*\). A direct expression of this idea is the naïve kernel estimator

\[ \hat{y}_{\text{naïve}}(x_*) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} k(x_*, x_i)\, y_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} k(x_*, x_j)} \]

which is a similarity-weighted average of training targets. This is in fact the classical *Nadaraya–Watson* estimator, and it requires no training at all—only storing the data. Its connection to KNN is immediate: KNN with a hard neighbourhood kernel is a special case where the sum averages only over the nearest neighbours.

Limitations of the naïve estimator.

The naïve estimator has two noteworthy limitations.

First, its weighting is *local and per-query*: the weights \(k(x_*, x_i)\) depend on the query point \(x_*\). In other words, the naïve estimator does not fit the ERM framework: there is no shared objective over the training data whose minimizer is \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

Second, when training points cluster in some region of the input space, each cluster member independently contributes a kernel-weighted vote—so denser regions naturally receive more total influence. If two training points \(x_i \approx x_j\) are nearly identical, their contributions \(k(x_*, x_i)\) and \(k(x_*, x_j)\) separately add to the prediction. There is a principled way to account for this redundancy. One can perform regression with

\[ \hat{y}(x_*) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \, k(x_*, x_i), \qquad \boldsymbol{\alpha} = (K + \lambda I)^{-1} y \]

where \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\) is the \(N \times N\) Gram matrix encoding pairwise training-point similarities, and \(\lambda > 0\) is a regularization constant. We call this kernel ridge regression (KRR), which is a direct kernelized version of linear regression. At test time, prediction at any new \(x_*\) requires evaluating \(k(x_*, x_i)\) for all \(N\) training points; the weights \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) are obtained once by solving a linear system. We will derive this formula in a later section.

Kernel averaging: the prediction at \(x_*\) is a weighted combination of training outputs, where the weights encode both similarity to \(x_*\) and the redundancy structure among training points.

### Support Vector Machines (SVM)

As noted above, SVMs sit at the boundary of the two routes. At test time they behave like a data-centric model: the prediction is anchored to a subset of training points and cannot be evaluated without them. At training time, however, they behave like a parametric model: an explicit optimization problem must be solved to determine *which* training points to retain and with what weights. That optimization encodes a geometric criterion—maximum margin—which we now state precisely.

Key idea.

The following formulation uses technical jargon (margin, hyperplane, dual form). If you are not familiar with these, please refer to a textbook for a detailed introduction and focus on the big picture: how the model structure places SVMs between the two routes.

For binary classification with labels \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\), the SVM seeks a maximum-margin separating hyperplane. In dual form, this becomes

\[ \max_{\alpha} \;\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j k(x_i, x_j) \quad \text{s.t. } 0 \le \alpha_i \le C, \;\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \]

The solution is sparse: only a subset of training points—the support vectors—have \(\alpha_i > 0\).

SVM: the decision boundary is determined by the support vectors (circled points), which are the training samples closest to the boundary.

Where SVMs sit in the two-route picture.

SVMs genuinely sit between the two routes. On the data-centric side: the final predictor is expressed as a kernel expansion over a subset of training points (the support vectors), so the training data is not fully discarded after learning—prediction still consults the stored support vectors. On the parametric side: an explicit optimization problem must be solved during training, and the margin criterion actively *selects* which data points survive as the carriers of the decision function. The result is a sparse, compressed representation of the training set, rather than either a fixed-size parameter vector (fully parametric) or the full training set retained verbatim (fully data-centric). Notably, the optimization above is *convex*, so it is not the iterative gradient search that characterizes parametric deep networks—it has a unique global solution reachable by dedicated solvers such as SMO.

The data-centric models (KNN, KRR, SVM) share a common structural property: the trained model retains an explicit dependency on the training data. Whether via neighbour lookup, kernel evaluations over all \(N\) support points, or a selection of support vectors, prediction cannot be carried out without consulting (some part of) the training set. This is a fundamental architectural feature, not merely a practical inconvenience: the training data *is* the model.

The parametric route (linear regression being the simplest case) chooses a different philosophy. The learning algorithm compresses the information in \(N\) examples into a fixed-size parameter vector \(\theta^*\). After training, the architecture is self-contained and the data is no longer needed. This compression is both the strength of the approach (constant-cost prediction, easy deployment) and its bottleneck (the representational power is capped by the expressivity of the chosen hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\)).

For rich hypothesis spaces—in particular deep neural networks where \(d\) can be in the billions—the normal equation is computationally infeasible. The dominant tool then is gradient descent and its many variants, which will be the subject of the next sections.

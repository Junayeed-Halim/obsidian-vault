# Real-valued Targets: Mean Squared Error vs. Mean Absolute Error

When dealing with regression tasks, the target variables lie within the real number domain, and the loss function simply measures the discrepancy between two real numbers. The most natural and widely utilized distance metric is the Squared Error (SE),

\[ L(y, f(x)) = (y - f(x))^2 \]

when this is evaluated on every instance in a dataset and averaged, it is commonly referred to as the Mean Squared Error (MSE). The "average over instances" part will be covered in the next section. Here to discuss the significance of this instance-level loss function, we will focus on the properties of the error definition itself, and you just need to keep in mind that "this will be applied to every instance" when going through the following discussion (including the absolute error).

Due to its smooth and convex geometric properties, being differentiable everywhere, MSE allows gradient-based optimization algorithms to iterate highly efficiently, and even permits the existence of closed-form solutions in certain simple linear models. Hence it is often the default choice for regression problems in building learning models.

There are a few interesting mathematical properties of the MSE loss function that are worth noting. First, this function is naturally connected to probabilistic generative models. Recall that the Gaussian distribution is defined

\[ \mathcal{N}(y; y_0, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(y-y_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

To simplify, let use \(\sigma=1\) for now and the distribution reads: "The probability density of observing \(y\) is proportional to \(\exp\left(-\frac{(y-y_0)^2}{2}\right)\), where \(y_0\) is the mean of the distribution."

This translates to the following motivation for learning: "According to a hypothesis \(f: X \mapsto Y\), the observed \(y\) for an input \(x\) (note that lowercase represents specific values taken by random variables and uppercase represents the random variable themselves) should be expected to be close to \(y_0 = f(x)\), when \(y\) deviates from \(f(x)\), the probability of observing such a \(y\) decreases exponentially with the square of the deviation \((y - f(x))^2\).

Now that I have already observed \(y\) for a given \(x\), this fact can be used to check the opinion that \(f\) is really the underlying hypothesis that generates the data."

This line of reasoning leads to the Bayesian inference framework to derive the likely hypothesis \(f\) given the observed data.

## Sensitivity to Outliers

One of the most important practical considerations when using MSE is its sensitivity to outliers. This is best understood by an intuitive geometric interpretation:

Figure 1: Geometric interpretation of MSE and MAE

It can be seen from the figure that the MSE loss is putting much more emphasis on larger errors. It actually forbid the model to fit to the main body of the data if there are extreme outliers.

If this is not the behaviour that we want, an alternative to the mean squared error, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is another common choice, defined as \(L(y, f(x)) = |y - f(x)|\). Extending further from a statistical perspective, minimizing the mean absolute error is strictly equivalent to the maximum likelihood estimation when assuming the noise in the data follows a Laplace distribution.

## Practical Considerations and Trade-offs

From the example you may have observed, these two loss functions exhibit distinct behavioral characteristics in engineering practice, and engineers must choose prudently based on the prior knowledge of the specific problem. The most relevant consideration in practice is their sensitivity to outliers.

The Mean Squared Error imposes a massive, quadratic penalty on larger errors. Consequently, if the dataset contains extreme outliers, the optimization process will severely deviate from the primary data distribution in an attempt to reduce the immense loss introduced by these single-point anomalies. In contrast, the Mean Absolute Error applies a linear penalty to errors, granting the model strong robustness against outliers.

However, despite the robustness of MAE, MSE is generally more favored in engineering. This is because its smoothness makes mathematical solving and backpropagation extremely straightforward. Conversely, the Mean Absolute Error is non-differentiable at the origin, which requires the use of subgradient techniques during the optimization process, thereby increasing the complexity of algorithm implementation.

Finally, the non-smooth nature of the MAE loss at the origin tends to guide the optimization process toward generating a sparser error distribution. In other words, a model trained with MAE tends to achieve perfect predictions (error exactly zero) on a subset of samples, while concentrating the remaining errors on samples that are difficult to fit.

This is often a desirable property in practice, as it allows certain interpretability of the resultant model. Therefore, sometime we may directly impose an MAE loss on the parameters of the model (even they have nothing to do with the error between prediction and target!) in order to encourage the model to produce a sparse parameter distribution. This trick is called \(L_1\) regularization. Regularization as a general concept is very useful in machine learning and will be discussed later.

# Empirical Risk

We have established that for a given hypothesis \(h\), the true risk \(E_{out}[h]\) can be approximated *efficiently and effectively* using training samples, even when the input space \(\mathcal{X}\) is high-dimensional. The loss aggregated over the training samples is called the empirical risk, \(E_{in}[h; D]\). \(E_{in}\) serves as a practical proxy for the true risk \(E_{out}\) in the learning process. We reproduce the approximation statement using the new notation here:

\[ E_{in}[h; D] \xrightarrow{N\to \infty} E_{out}[h], \]

where \(N\) is the number of training samples. It is useful to recall that in the expression above, the core element that takes the limit with \(N \to \infty\) is a *probability measure* over the data space \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}\). With more and more training samples, the empirical distribution of the training data (with point masses at the training examples) converges to the true data distribution \(P_{Data}\). When the approximation between the two distributions is close enough, the integral of the loss function \(L(h(x), y)\) with respect to the two distributions will also be close, which underlies the statement above.

The remaining part of this module is a more detailed discussion of this approximation. We know that \(E_{in}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(E_{out}\) as \(N \to \infty\). The next natural question is: why? And how fast does the approximation converge — as \(N\) grows, how does the discrepancy between \(E_{in}\) and \(E_{out}\) decrease?

The complete formal discussion is a bit technical and depends on the specific setting of the learning problem (e.g., the loss function, the data distribution, the hypothesis, etc.). We will provide technical details for one simple but common scenario: the task is binary classification, and the loss is simply to count the fraction of misclassified examples (the error-counting loss):

\[ L(h(x), y) = \mathbb{I}\{h(x) \neq y\}, \]

where \(\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}\) is the indicator function that equals 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise. In this case, the true risk \(E_{out}[h]\) is simply the probability that \(h(x)\) disagrees with \(y\) when \((x,y)\) is drawn from the data distribution \(P_{Data}\):

\begin{align} E_{out}[h] = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim P_{Data}} \bigl[\mathbb{I}\{h(x) \neq y\}\bigr] = P_{Data}\bigl(h(x) \neq y\bigr). \label{eq:true_risk_binary} \end{align}

Note that if we take a simplistic view of the task, where there is a unique deterministic function \(f^*\) such that \(y = f^*(x)\), then the probability of a pair \((x,y)\) according to \(P_{Data}\) is the same as the probability of \(x\) according to the marginal distribution \(P_X\) (the distribution of \(x\)). This is because \(y\) is completely determined by \(x\) via \(f^*\), so the randomness in \((x,y)\) comes solely from the randomness in \(x\). We will adopt this simplified view for the sake of intuition, and write \(P_{Data}(h(x) \neq y)\) as \(P_X(h(x) \neq f^*(x))\), and be concerned with the samples \(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N\) empirically approximating the distribution \(P_X\) over \(\mathcal{X}\).

The probability of \(h\) making a mistake when processing new data, can be interpreted as the size of a “bad region” in the input space \(\mathcal{X}\) where \(h\) and \(f^*(x)\) disagree. The empirical risk in this case is the fraction of training examples on which \(h\) disagrees with the labels:

\[ E_{in}[h; D] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}\{h(x_i) \neq y_i\}. \]

It can be considered as a discretized estimate of the “bad region”. What we want to establish is that the true size and the discretized counting are close with high probability.

However, although the above picture seems intuitive, it is not tractable to directly measure regions in the space \(\mathcal{X}\). Before proceeding, it is worth pausing to appreciate a subtle but important shift in perspective — we will be investigating a more abstract space, where the atomic objects are datasets of \(N\) independently drawn samples from \(\mathcal{X}\), i.e., a *single point* in this new space, \(\Omega\), is a dataset of \(N\) samples. As \(N\) varies, these atomic objects differ in size, i.e., they are datasets of different cardinalities. Hence we often denote this space as \(\Omega_N\).

By paying the price of introducing this new space of random datasets, we can now treat \(E_{in}\) as a random variable defined on \(\Omega_N\), and we can apply the powerful tools of probability theory to quantify the approximation \(E_{in} \approx E_{out}\), and to understand how the quality of the approximation depends on \(N\).

Concretely, the probabilistic question we wish to answer is: how likely is it that \(E_{in}\) deviates from \(E_{out}\) by more than a prescribed tolerance \(\epsilon > 0\), i.e., what is \(P(|E_{in} - E_{out}| > \epsilon)\)? In this expression, \(E_{in}\) and \(E_{out}\) play asymmetric roles. Note that with \(h\) fixed, \(E_{out}\) becomes a single (albeit unknown) number—it is a deterministic constant in our context. The “how likely” is a probability over the random draw of the training set \(D\), i.e., it refers to events in the space \(\Omega_N\) of datasets.

## PAC Guarantee for a Fixed \(h\): Hoeffding’s Inequality

We have argued informally that \(E_{in}[D]\) concentrates around \(E_{out}\) as \(N\) grows. But how do we make this precise and quantify our confidence? The key tool is Hoeffding’s inequality.

The bound. For any tolerance \(\epsilon > 0\), Hoeffding’s inequality gives:

\begin{align} \boxed{ P\!\bigl(|E_{in}[D] - E_{out}| > \epsilon\bigr) \;\le\; 2\exp(-2N\epsilon^2). } \label{eq:hoeffding} \end{align}

Interpretation. Recall that we introduced the space \(\Omega_N\) of datasets precisely so that \(E_{in}\) could be treated as a random variable. The Hoeffding's bound now gives this abstraction its payoff: the probability on the left-hand side is a measure on \(\Omega_N\), and the event \(\{|E_{in}[D] - E_{out}| > \epsilon\}\) is the subset of “bad” datasets in \(\Omega_N\) — those for which the empirical risk gives a misleading picture of the true risk. Hoeffding’s inequality bounds the *size* of this bad subset.

Two features of the bound are worth highlighting. First, it is *distribution-free*: the right-hand side does not depend on the data distribution \(P\), the dimension \(d\) of \(\mathcal{X}\), or the specific form of \(h\). This confirms formally our earlier claim that the quality of the approximation \(E_{in} \approx E_{out}\) is governed by \(N\) alone. Second, the bound decays *exponentially* in \(N\), which is much faster than, say, the \(1/\sqrt{N}\) rate one might expect from the central limit theorem. This exponential decay is why even moderate sample sizes can yield strong guarantees.

To build intuition, consider a few concrete numbers. Suppose we want \(E_{in}\) to be within \(\epsilon = 0.05\) of \(E_{out}\). We call a training set \(D\) “bad” if it gives an \(E_{in}[D]\) that is more than 5 percentage points away from \(E_{out}\). Hoeffding’s inequality says that the probability of drawing such a bad training set is at most:

\[ 2\exp(-2N \cdot 0.05^2) = 2\exp(-0.005\,N). \]

With \(N = 1000\) training samples, this bound evaluates to \(2e^{-5} \approx 0.013\), meaning there is *at most* a 1.3% chance that our empirical risk is misleading us by more than 5 percentage points. With \(N = 2000\), the bound drops to \(2e^{-10} \approx 0.00009\) — a virtually negligible probability. The exponential decay in \(N\) also tells us something practically useful: to halve the tolerance \(\epsilon\) while maintaining the same confidence level, we need to quadruple the sample size (since the exponent scales as \(N\epsilon^2\)).

Intuitively, Hoeffding’s inequality says that once a sufficiently large training set has rendered a verdict \(E_{in}\) on hypothesis \(h\), it is exceedingly unlikely that this verdict will be overturned by the true performance \(E_{out}\) on unseen data.

General Form and Derivation of the Bound. Hoeffding’s inequality is a general concentration result for bounded random variables. For any i.i.d. sequence \(Z_1, \dots, Z_N\) taking values in a bounded interval \([a, b]\):

\begin{align} P\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i - \mathbb{E}[Z]\right| > \epsilon\right) \;\le\; 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2N\epsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}\right). \label{eq:hoeffding_general} \end{align}

To apply this to our setting, define, for each training example \((x_i, y_i)\), the random variable

\[ Z_i := \mathbb{I}\{h(x_i) \neq y_i\} \in \{0, 1\}, \]

which is the instance-level \(0\)-\(1\) loss: it equals \(1\) if \(h\) misclassifies the \(i\)-th example and \(0\) otherwise. Since \(Z_i \in \{0,1\}\) we have \(b - a = 1\), and \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i Z_i = E_{in}[h;D]\) while \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = E_{out}[h]\). Substituting into the general Hoeffding's inequality immediately yields the desired bound for Ein.

# Dimensionality and Why Empirical Risk Still Works

We have established that a hypothesis \(h\) is evaluated over the input space \(\mathcal{X}\), and at each point, the instance loss \(L\) compares \(h(x)\) with the true system's reaction \(f^*(x)\). Because the *mechanism* \(f^*\) is unknown, and we have only a finite observation of how \(f^*\) reacts to some example points \(x_i\)'s, we have to approximate the evaluation by

\[ \underbrace{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N L\!\bigl(h(x_i), f^*(x_i)\bigr)}_{\text{empirical risk: } E_{in}[h;D]} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}} L\!\bigl(h(x), f^*(x)\bigr)\, dx}_{\text{total risk: } E_{\text{all}}[h]} \]
## The Curse of Dimensionality: The Naive Argument

To understand why we should worry about the quality of this approximation in high-dimensional input spaces, let us reflect on the basic philosophy of why learning is possible at all.

The discussion and debate on this topic can be traced back to ancient Greek philosophy. Thinkers across all traditions have grappled with questions such as: what constitutes knowledge, whether it can be acquired, and by what means. Among the many competing views, one piece of common ground — albeit modest — is that to extract principles applicable to future situations from past observations, the world “out there” must exhibit some degree of regularity: when a new situation closely resembles one encountered in the past, the outcome of interest is likely to be similar as well.

Here we want to be more specific about the notion “two situations are similar”. The “situation” in our context is a data sample, represented by the input variables \(x = [x_1, \dots, x_d]\). To one piece of experience, we can associate a small neighbourhood in the input space, within which similarity can be declared. That is, for a given sample \(x^{\mathrm{ref}}\), if another sample \(x'\) falls within the neighbourhood of \(x^{\mathrm{ref}}\),

\[ \mathrm{Dist}(x',\, x^{\mathrm{ref}}) \;\le\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{Similar}}, \]

then \(x'\) is similar to \(x^{\mathrm{ref}}\), and we want to propagate the outcome at \(x^{\mathrm{ref}}\) to predict that for \(x'\).

Now we can comprehend the problem of learning in high-dimensional spaces. The volume in which an individual sample \(x^{\mathrm{ref}}\) can represent (i.e.\ the neighbourhood of similarity as above) shrinks rapidly as the dimensionality increases.

To put it concretely, if we let the representative volume be of radius \(1\) — i.e. a unit sphere — then the volume of this sphere in \(d\)-dimensional space is surprisingly small compared to the volume of, say, a “box” of the ambient space. The figure below shows the sphere in 1D, 2D, and 3D:

Also be reminded that the radius we have chosen here is GENEROUS, and the ambient space is SMALL — a TOUCHING cube, whose volume is \(2^d\) (Why?). This seems to be the most favourable picture of “using samples to represent the space”, and yet the picture is already bleak. The ratio is

\[ \frac{\text{Volume of unit sphere}}{\text{Volume of cube}} = \frac{\pi^{d/2} / \Gamma(d/2 + 1)}{2^d} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi d}} \left(\frac{\pi e}{2d}\right)^{d/2}, \]

which shrinks super-exponentially as \(d\) increases.

This is the well-known curse of dimensionality.

## The Rescue: Concentration of Measure and Law of Large Numbers

Fortunately, the naive argument above rests on a flawed premise: it tacitly assumes that \(h\) must be evaluated *uniformly* over the entire input space \(\mathcal{X}\). In reality, it is only meaningful to assess how \(h\) performs on the regions of \(\mathcal{X}\) where data is actually likely to occur. The claim that \(E_{in}\) fails to approximate \(E_{\text{all}}\) is technically true — but entirely beside the point, because \(E_{\text{all}}\) is not the quantity we care about. The true target is the expected risk (or generalisation error)

\[ \begin{aligned} E_{\text{out}}[h] &= \int_{\mathcal{X}} L\!\bigl(h(x), f^*(x)\bigr)\, d\,{\color{red}{P(x)}} \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\bigl[L(h(x), f^*(x))\bigr]. \end{aligned} \]

Here the integration is with respect to the probability measure \(P\) — the notation \(dP(x)\) should be read as: “at the point \(x\), over an infinitesimally small volume element \(\Delta V_x\), how much probability mass does \(P\) assign to \(\Delta V_x\)?” In other words, regions that are rarely visited by real data contribute negligibly to \(E_{\text{out}}\), regardless of how large they are in the geometric sense — they are just vast emptiness in the probability landscape. It turns out that with reasonably sized samples, we can approximate \(E_{\text{out}}\) well enough, even in very high-dimensional spaces.

To make this concrete, return to the image analytics example. The ambient space \(\mathcal{X}\) of all possible pixel configurations in a \(256 \times 256\) RGB image has dimension \(d = 196{,}608\). Yet the overwhelming majority of points in this space — chosen, say, by assigning each pixel a value uniformly at random — correspond to pure noise: patterns that would never appear in any photograph, drawing, or scan. The data-generating distribution \(P\) assigns essentially zero probability mass to these regions. From the perspective of \(E_{\text{out}}\), they simply do not exist. The “space” is geometrically vast, but probabilistically almost empty. What matters is not how large \(\mathcal{X}\) is, but where \(P\) places its mass — and in practice, that mass is concentrated on a comparatively tiny, structured region of \(\mathcal{X}\).

Let us recap and clarify our standing here. We want to evaluate the quality of a hypothesis \(h\). Now with \(h\) fixed, consider the pointwise risk \(r(x) := L(h(x), f^*(x))\), named \(r(x)\) to emphasise that it is a function of \(x\) (\(h\) is fixed). We want to know the integrated risk over the *population* (rather than the entire space) of \(\mathcal{X}\), that is, \(E_{\text{out}}[h] = \int_{\mathcal{X}} r(x)\, dP(x)\). The question then becomes: can we approximate this expectation by drawing samples from \(P\) and averaging the function values at those samples? This is precisely the question of *Monte Carlo integration*, and the answer is yes for a wide class of functions — for virtually all instance losses (\(r(x)\)) encountered in practice.

The validity of our evaluation of \(h\) rests on a classical result from probability theory: the law of large numbers (LLN). The LLN states that the sample average \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i\) of i.i.d.\ random variables \(Z_i\) converges to the true mean \(\mathbb{E}[Z]\) as \(N \to \infty\). More precisely, the empirical measure \(\hat{P}_N := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{x_i}\) — which assigns equal mass \(1/N\) to each observed sample — converges *weakly* to the true data-generating distribution \(P\). Weak convergence here means: for any “nice” test function \(f\), putting the two measures at work (i.e.\ by using them to integrate \(f\)), the two integrals are close to each other:

\[ \int f(x)\, d\hat{P}_N(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i) \;\longrightarrow\; \int f(x)\, dP(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[f(x)]. \]

Taking \(f(x) = L(h(x), f^*(x)) = r(x)\) immediately gives us \(E_{in}[h] \to E_{\text{out}}[h]\). The function \(f\) is “nice” if it does not explode or oscillate too wildly; the instance losses we encounter in practice comfortably satisfy this condition.

Concentration of measure is the probabilistic tool that underpins the LLN and, crucially, provides *finite-sample* guarantees. It tells us how quickly the sample average concentrates around its true mean as \(N\) grows — that is, how large \(N\) must be before \(E_{in}\) is reliably close to \(E_{\text{out}}\).

The key point: the quality of the approximation \(E_{in} \approx E_{\text{out}}\) is governed by how many training examples we have, and is *independent of the ambient dimension* \(d\) of each sample \(x_i\).

Summary of the argument. More formally, the curse of dimensionality applies to *grid-based or uniform-coverage* approaches to integration, which require exponentially many points to cover a \(d\)-dimensional cube. Empirical risk minimisation is neither of these things. It is a *sample-based* approximation whose quality depends on:

- The number of samples \(N\) relative to the complexity of \(\mathcal{H}\).
- The concentration properties of \(P\) (how “spread out” the data is).
- The smoothness of \(h\) (how rapidly \(L(h(x), y)\) changes with \(x\)).

None of these factors is directly determined by the ambient dimension \(d\). The key is that the natural data-generating process of the world provides samples in exactly the high-probability regions of \(\mathcal{X}\), which is precisely where accurate approximation matters.

## A Concrete Example: Linear Classifiers on the Sphere

To see the Law of Large Numbers at work, consider the following setup. Let the input space be the unit sphere \(\mathcal{S}^{d-1} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\), and suppose both the true system \(f^*\) and a candidate hypothesis \(h\) are linear threshold functions (perceptrons):

\[ f^*(x) = \operatorname{sign}(\langle w^*, x \rangle), \qquad h(x) = \operatorname{sign}(\langle w, x \rangle), \]

where \(w^*, w \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are fixed weight vectors. The instance loss is the 0–1 loss: \(L(h(x), f^*(x)) = \mathbf{1}\{h(x) \neq f^*(x)\}\).

The true risk has a closed form. When \(x\) is drawn uniformly from \(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\), the two half-spaces \(\{\langle w^*, x\rangle > 0\}\) and \(\{\langle w, x\rangle > 0\}\) disagree on a fraction of the sphere that equals the normalised angle between the two weight vectors:

\[ E_{\text{out}}[h] = \frac{\angle(w^*, w)}{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{\langle w^*, w \rangle}{\|w^*\|\,\|w\|}\right). \]

This gives us a ground truth to compare against.

The empirical risk. Given \(N\) i.i.d.\ samples \(x_1, \dots, x_N\) drawn from the uniform distribution on the sphere, the empirical risk is simply the fraction of samples on which the two classifiers disagree:

\[ E_{in}[h; D] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}\!\bigl\{ \operatorname{sign}(\langle w^*, x_i\rangle) \neq \operatorname{sign}(\langle w, x_i\rangle) \bigr\}. \]

What happens as \(N\) grows. The figure below shows the distribution of \(E_{in}\) over 100 independent trials, for \(N \in \{10, 50, 100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000\}\), for a pair of linear classifiers in \(\mathbb{R}^{5}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{100}\) respectively. The red horizontal line is the true risk \(E_{\text{out}}\).

Distribution of the empirical risk \(E_{in}\) as a function of sample size \(N\), for a pair of linear classifiers in \(\mathbb{R}^{5}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{100}\), respectively. Each box summarises 100 independent trials. As \(N\) increases, the boxes tighten around the true risk \(E_{\text{out}}\) (red line), confirming the Law of Large Numbers. Crucially, the rate of concentration depends only on \(N\), not on the ambient dimension \(d\).

Two observations are worth emphasising:

- **Convergence is rapid.** Already at \(N = 1000\), the spread of \(E_{in}\) around \(E_{\text{out}}\) is small enough to be practically negligible.
- **Dimension does not appear.** Repeating the experiment with \(d = 5\) or \(d = 1000\) yields virtually identical box widths for the same \(N\). The ambient dimension of each sample \(x_i\) is irrelevant to the quality of the approximation \(E_{in} \approx E_{\text{out}}\).

This is the central message: the curse of dimensionality does *not* affect empirical risk minimisation, because the approximation error is controlled by the number of samples \(N\), not by the dimension \(d\) of the input space.

Once the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) is parametrized by a vector \(\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\)—meaning every function \(h \in \mathcal{H}\) is realized as \(h_\theta\) for some \(\theta\)—the ERM problem becomes the scalar optimization

\[\begin{align} \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta}\; J(\theta), \qquad J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L\bigl(h_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\bigr). \label{eq:param_erm} \end{align}\]

The most direct approach to \eqref{eq:param_erm} would be analytic: set \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = 0\) and solve the resulting system of equations. This is what we did for linear regression in the introduction section. However, this is infeasible for any model that composes more than one nonlinear function, and thereby motivates the iterative procedure that occupies the rest of this module. The next subsection explains why the analytic approach fails for a simple two-layer network. It is technical and shows the point with formality; feel free to skip it on first reading and just take the point that analytic solutions are not available for general tasks.

Be aware of a notational convention: we will use \(\theta\) to denote the vector of all parameters in a model, and use \(J(\theta)\) to denote the loss over all training samples as a function of \(\theta\). The symbol \(\theta\) will subsume more specific references such as weights \(w\) and constants \(b\) in a neural network, and \(J(\theta)\) is just a shorthand for the \(\theta\)-dependency in \(E_{\mathrm{in}}[h(\,\cdot\,;\theta);\, \mathrm{Data}]\).

## No Analytic Solution

Consider the two-layer network for the XOR classification problem. Given input \(x = (x_1, x_2)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the network computes

\[\begin{align} z^{(1)} &= W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}, \quad h = \sigma(z^{(1)}), \label{eq:layer1} \\ z^{(2)} &= (w^{(2)})^\top h + b^{(2)}, \quad \hat{y} = \sigma(z^{(2)}), \label{eq:layer2} \end{align}\]

where \(W^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}\), \(b^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^2\), \(w^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^2\), \(b^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) applies coordinate-wise. On a single training example \((x, y)\) with \(y \in \{0,1\}\), the binary cross-entropy loss is

\[\begin{align} L(\hat{y}, y) = -y \log \hat{y} - (1-y) \log(1 - \hat{y}). \label{eq:bce} \end{align}\]

This network has \(4 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 9\) scalar parameters in total (see examples/demo5_2_no_explicit.ipynb for the full computation graph built via PyTorch autograd).

To minimize \(L\) analytically we would set \(\partial L / \partial W^{(1)} = 0\) and solve for \(W^{(1)}\). Applying the chain rule gives

\[\begin{align} \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(1)}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}}}_{\hat{y} - y} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z^{(2)}}}_{\sigma'(z^{(2)})} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(2)}}{\partial h}}_{w^{(2)}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{(1)}}}_{\mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(z^{(1)}))} \cdot x^\top. \label{eq:chain} \end{align}\]

The obstacle is that \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\) is a nonlinear function of \(z\), which is itself a nonlinear function of the parameters. Setting \eqref{eq:chain} to zero therefore produces a system of coupled transcendental equations in all nine parameters simultaneously—equations with no closed form. Because this obstruction appears at every layer of any network with sigmoid activations, there is no analytic minimizer for \(J(\theta)\) in general. The only viable strategy is to search *iteratively*: start from some initial \(\theta_0\) and repeatedly compute a local approximation of \(J\) to decide where to move next.

## Local Approximations and the Gradient Descent Update

The idea of “searching” and optimisation is thus formed by considering the local behaviour of \(J\) around the current point \(\theta_0\). By “behaviour” we mean “how \(J\) varies in response to small changes in \(\theta\)”.

The analysis proceeds by taking the first-order Taylor expansion of \(J\) around \(\theta_0\):

\[\begin{align} J(\theta) \approx J(\theta_0) + \nabla_\theta J(\theta_0)^\top (\theta - \theta_0). \label{eq:taylor1} \end{align}\]

Among all directions, \(J\) decreases most steeply along \(-\nabla_\theta J(\theta_0)\), so we move a small distance \(\eta > 0\) in that direction and obtain a new point \(\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta_0)\). Applying the same prescription at \(\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots\) defines gradient descent:

\[\begin{align} \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta_t), \quad t = 0,1,2,\ldots \label{eq:gd} \end{align}\]

The scalar \(\eta > 0\) is the size of this small step, and is commonly called the learning rate. Usually this is a hyperparameter that we set to a small positive value.

### The Geometric View

The steepest descent direction has a nice geometric interpretation. If there are \(p\) parameters, then \((\theta, J(\theta))\) is a surface in \(\mathbb{R}^{p+1}\). (See the figure below for the case \(p=2\) to have a concrete impression.)

For a model with exactly two parameters \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\), \(J\) is a real-valued surface over the plane and can be plotted directly. Gradient descent \eqref{eq:gd} corresponds to repeatedly moving downhill on this surface: at step \(t\) we stand at \((\theta_{t,1},\, \theta_{t,2},\, J(\theta_t))\) on the surface, read off the local slope \(\nabla J(\theta_t)\), and take a step of length \(\eta\) in the direction of steepest descent. The sequence \(\theta_0, \theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots\) thereby traces a trajectory across the surface heading toward a minimum. ## Steps in Gradient Descent

It goes without saying that the essential ingredient of gradient descent is to compute the gradient \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) given the current \(\theta\). This main computational task depends on the specific model, and will receive detailed treatment in the study of neural networks (backpropagation) later. Here we will discuss a methodologically orthogonal aspect that is seemingly trivial but is actually quite subtle: choosing the step sizes.

The local geometry of \(\nabla J\) tells us about the behaviour of the loss at an *infinitesimal* scale, i.e. throwing away all higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion is only 100% justified when taking the change to the limit of 0, \(\Delta \theta \to 0\). However, infinitesimality is not a practical choice. It must be compromised with finite step sizes. In the previous discussion, we have overlooked this issue by assuming a small positive \(\eta\).

In practice, setting an appropriate \(\eta\) is an important and non-trivial problem. Various strategies have been developed to address it, and the choice of \(\eta\) can have a significant impact on the convergence and performance of the optimization process. We will discuss some of these strategies later in this module. For now, let us start with a toy example that illustrates some common phenomena related to practical optimisation steps.

### The Perceptron as Gradient Descent with \(\eta = 1\)

The toy model is a binary classifier called the perceptron, which is just a linear (single-layer) neural network with a sign activation function (the model outputs \(+1\) or \(-1\) depending on the sign of the linear combination of inputs).

Formally, the perceptron classifies \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) via

\[ h(x) = \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x), \]

where \(w \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the weight vector. A perceptron learning rule is an iterative procedure that updates \(w\) based on the misclassifications on the training data. Whenever a training example \((x_i, y_i)\) with \(y_i \in \{+1,-1\}\) is *misclassified*, i.e.\ \(y_i(w^\top x_i) \leq 0\), the weight vector is updated by

\[\begin{align} w \leftarrow w + y_i\,x_i. \label{eq:perceptron} \end{align}\]

Considering that the gradient of the *pre-activation* \(z_i = w^\top x_i\) with respect to \(w\) is just \(x_i\), and \(z_i\) of a misclassified point contributes to the loss by \(1\) (taking the counting loss as the loss function), one may consider the update as following a “gradient descent” direction with \(\eta = 1\).

It turns out that when the data are linearly separable—meaning there exists \(w^*\) such that \(y_i(w^{*\top} x_i) > 0\) for all \(i\)—the Perceptron Convergence Theorem (Novikoff, 1962) guarantees that \eqref{eq:perceptron} terminates after at most \((R/\gamma)^2\) updates, where \(R = \max_i \|x_i\|\) and \(\gamma = \min_i y_i(w^{*\top} x_i/\|w^*\|)\) is the geometric margin of the separating hyperplane. Termination is certain, yet the intermediate iterates may oscillate substantially before settling: the unit step is not calibrated to the margin \(\gamma\), so even a wide margin does not prevent oscillation.

When the data are not linearly separable, the fixed unit step causes the algorithm to never terminate. There always remain irreducibly misclassified points, and the boundary is pushed back and forth among them indefinitely.

The perceptron example makes concrete a problem that persists in all gradient-based learning: when the step size is fixed and not tuned to the local geometry of the loss, the iterates oscillate. Even in the favorable case (separable data), the path to convergence is erratic; in the unfavorable case (non-separable data), the algorithm never terminates at all.

Part of the difficulty with the perceptron is that its loss—the count of misclassified points—is a discrete, piecewise-constant function of \(w\). A continuous and differentiable loss function would at least allow the gradient magnitude to shrink as the parameters approach a region of low loss, providing a natural damping effect that the counting loss lacks entirely. The logistic (cross-entropy) loss has exactly this property.

### Continuous Loss (Logistic)

In a more realistic setting, in logistic regression the loss on a single example is \(L = -y_i \log \sigma(w^\top x_i) - (1-y_i)\log(1-\sigma(w^\top x_i))\), and its gradient with respect to the linear output \(z = w^\top x_i\) is

\[\begin{align} \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \sigma(z) - y_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i. \label{eq:logistic_grad} \end{align}\]

As the prediction \(\hat{y}_i\) approaches the label \(y_i\), the gradient \eqref{eq:logistic_grad} approaches zero. The gradient-descent step therefore becomes progressively smaller near a minimum without any explicit schedule, which substantially reduces the risk of oscillation compared to the perceptron.

This automatic damping adjusts *relative* magnitudes as training progresses, but does not determine the overall scale of \(\eta\). The simplest choice is to fix \(\eta\) to a constant selected before training, typically by observing the rate at which the loss decreases on a held-out set.

- in the early stage, a large \(\eta\) jumps across the loss landscape where one can safely ignore the higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion, and thus leads to a more erratic trajectory;
- in the later stage, a large \(\eta\) may cause the trajectory to overshoot the minimum and oscillate around it, or even diverge away from it.

### Second-Order Approximation

The fundamental limitation of the first-order expansion \eqref{eq:taylor1} is that it says nothing about *how far* the gradient direction remains a good descent direction—it prescribes a direction but not a distance. A second-order Taylor expansion supplies this missing information:

\[\begin{align} J(\theta) \approx J(\theta_0) + \nabla J(\theta_0)^\top (\theta - \theta_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_0)^\top H(\theta_0)\,(\theta - \theta_0), \label{eq:taylor2} \end{align}\]

where \(H(\theta_0) = \nabla^2_\theta J(\theta_0) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}\) is the matrix of all second partial derivatives of \(J\) at \(\theta_0\). The quadratic \eqref{eq:taylor2}, unlike the linear \eqref{eq:taylor1}, has an analytic minimizer—set its gradient to zero and solve:

\[\begin{align} \theta_1 = \theta_0 - H(\theta_0)^{-1}\,\nabla J(\theta_0). \label{eq:newton} \end{align}\]

In this update the step size is determined by the curvature: in directions where \(J\) curves steeply (large eigenvalue of \(H\)) the step is small, and in flat directions it is large. No separate \(\eta\) is required. The price is that forming \(H\) requires \(O(p^2)\) storage and inverting it costs \(O(p^3)\) arithmetic, which is prohibitive for any model with more than a few thousand parameters. Approximations that retain useful curvature information at affordable cost will be discussed in later sections.

## Delegating Second-Order Information to the Learning Rate

In practice, the most common approach is to stick with the first-order expansion \eqref{eq:taylor1} but to choose \(\eta\) adaptively at each step.

The simplest heuristic is to decay \(\eta\) according to a fixed schedule, such as \(\eta_t = \eta_0 / \sqrt{t}\), which ensures that the step size shrinks as training progresses, but does not adapt to the local geometry of \(J\).

A more principled option is line search: at each step \(t\), choose \(\eta_t\) by (approximately) minimizing \(J(\theta_t - \eta\,\nabla J(\theta_t))\) as a function of the scalar \(\eta\) alone. Methods that assign a separate, adaptively updated learning rate to each parameter—using the history of past gradients to estimate appropriate scales—will be studied in subsequent sections.

However, we end this section with a cautionary note. Every strategy discussed above—fixed \(\eta\), decay schedules, line search, even the second-order update \eqref{eq:newton}—shares the same fundamental character: at each step the algorithm reads the local gradient (and possibly local curvature) at the current \(\theta_t\) and moves to a nearby point with lower loss. The algorithm is locally greedy, and a locally greedy procedure is only guaranteed to reach a point where no small move reduces \(J\) further—a *local* minimum, not necessarily the global one.

## Local Minima and Non-Convexity

Whether gradient descent finds a *global* minimizer of \(J\) depends on whether \(J\) is convex. \(J\) is convex if for every \(\theta, \theta' \in \Theta\) and every \(\lambda \in [0,1]\),

\[\begin{align} J\bigl(\lambda\theta + (1-\lambda)\theta'\bigr) \leq \lambda J(\theta) + (1-\lambda)J(\theta'). \label{eq:convex} \end{align}\]

Its key consequence is that every stationary point \(\theta^*\) satisfying \(\nabla J(\theta^*) = 0\) is a global minimizer, so gradient descent with a sufficiently small \(\eta\) converges to the global optimum regardless of where it starts. Logistic regression with a linear model satisfies \eqref{eq:convex}, but the two-layer XOR network does not.

For a non-convex \(J\), a stationary point need not be a global minimizer. Gradient descent converges to whichever local minimum is closest to the starting point \(\theta_0\), and which minimum that is depends entirely on \(\theta_0\). The figure above shows this for the XOR network: the loss surface has multiple valleys, and which one the optimizer enters is determined by initialization, not by the quality of the optimizer.

A particularly problematic type of stationary point is a saddle point: a point \(\theta\) where \(\nabla J(\theta) = 0\) and the Hessian \(H(\theta)\) has at least one positive and at least one negative eigenvalue. At a saddle point the gradient vanishes and gradient descent stalls, yet the point is not a local minimum—there exist directions along which \(J\) decreases further. For wide neural networks, empirical and theoretical evidence suggests that most stationary points encountered during training are saddle points rather than poor local minima, making saddle-point escape the central challenge. Addressing saddle points requires mechanisms beyond locally greedy descent—perturbations, accumulated momentum, or stochastic noise introduced by mini-batch sampling—all of which are studied in subsequent sections.
